\title{Co-Scheduled Adjoint Greeks in a Fixed-Point FPGA Datapath:\\ Heston-Model Option Sensitivities with First-Order Rounding Bounds}

\author{\IEEEauthorblockN{Yash Agrawal}
\IEEEauthorblockA{Birla Institute of Technology and Science, Pilani\\ Pilani, India}
\and
\IEEEauthorblockN{Govind Prasad}
\IEEEauthorblockA{Birla Institute of Technology and Science, Pilani\\ Pilani, India}}

\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
FPGA option-pricing engines obtain Greeks, the sensitivities of a price to its inputs,
by bump-and-reprice or by re-simulation. We present an FPGA engine for European options
under the Heston model, priced with the COS Fourier-cosine method, in which a
hand-derived reverse (adjoint) sweep is co-scheduled with the forward pass in one
statically scheduled fixed-point datapath. Because the COS loop has no data-dependent
control flow, the values the reverse sweep needs are held in a statically allocated,
register-resident tape rather than in memory. A generator emits, from one operation
graph, a bit-accurate model, the modulo-scheduled Verilog and a first-order rounding-error
bound for the price and each of nine first-order Greeks, used at design time to size and
certify the word length. Implemented in Vivado on a Zynq-7020 at equal throughput, the
price-and-Greeks engine takes 1.03$\times$ the cycles of a price-only engine at
1.68$\times$ its LUTs and 2$\times$ its DSP blocks; a factored forward-mode engine built by
the same flow needs 7\% more LUTs, 12\% more flip-flops and 25\% more DSP blocks and is
6$\times$ less accurate at the same word length. The bound was never exceeded over 21,000
inputs, and the engine ran bit-exact on two FPGA families (1,469/1,469 outputs, including
50 calibrations to NIFTY options). The bound covers rounding only; the COS truncation error
dominates in a few in-domain cases. The computed engine latency on a ZCU102, 15.9\,$\mu$s
excluding bus transfers, is 4.9--15$\times$ below general-purpose AD and bump-and-reprice
on a laptop core but not below a vectorised implementation of hand-derived Greeks
(\SIMDUS\,$\mu$s).
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Option Greeks, the partial derivatives of a price with respect to spot, strike,
maturity, rate and the model's parameters, set hedge ratios and risk limits and drive
model calibration. Under the Heston stochastic-volatility model~\cite{heston} the price
has no closed form and is computed numerically, here by the COS method~\cite{fang}.

On FPGAs, published engines compute Greeks by bump-and-reprice or by re-running the
simulation for each sensitivity~\cite{klai,klaih2rc,vitisquant}. Nine Greeks by central
differences cost 19 pricings, and the result depends on a bump size with no setting that
is safe for every Greek (Fig.~\ref{fig:bump}). Software replaced bumping with adjoint
algorithmic differentiation (AAD)~\cite{giles,capriotti,capgiles24}, which returns the
derivatives of one output with respect to all inputs in a single reverse sweep, at the
cost of recording a tape of the forward computation; our taped CPU baseline costs 7.6
pricings.

The reverse sweep of a computation with no data-dependent control flow can be scheduled
statically, a property exploited by source-transformation AD~\cite{tapenade} and by
neural-network training accelerators. The COS pricer has this property: every one of its
128 terms runs the same operations. We use it to co-schedule a hand-derived reverse sweep
with the forward pass in one fixed-point datapath, and ask what the Greeks then cost in
time, area and accuracy, measured against the alternatives built through the same flow.
The contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{A co-scheduled adjoint datapath} for Heston--COS Greeks, in which forward
values live in a statically allocated, register-resident tape (Section~\ref{sec:method}).
After a search of the FPGA and finance-acceleration literature
(Section~\ref{sec:background}) we know of no prior FPGA computation of option Greeks by an
adjoint, and no prior FPGA implementation of the COS method.
\item \textbf{A measured cost of the Greeks}: three engines of equal throughput (price
only, adjoint, factored forward mode) implemented in Vivado through the same generator
and flow, with resources, cycles and fixed-point accuracy side by side
(Section~\ref{sec:res-cost}).
\item \textbf{A first-order rounding-error bound for every Greek} of a datapath that
contains its own reverse sweep, extending AD-based precision analysis for
FPGAs~\cite{gaffar,lee06} to a datapath that itself differentiates; with the scope of the
bound, the modelling of the arithmetic primitives and its empirical validation stated
explicitly (Sections~\ref{sec:bound},~\ref{sec:res-acc}).
\item \textbf{A generator and bit-exact silicon results} on two FPGA families, including
calibrations to real market data (Section~\ref{sec:res-silicon}).
\end{enumerate}
What is not claimed: the reverse sweep is derived by hand, not by an AD tool; the bound
covers rounding only and is computed at design time, not by the engine; and the engine is
not faster than a vectorised CPU implementation of hand-derived Greeks
(Section~\ref{sec:discussion}).

\section{Background and Related Work}
\label{sec:background}
\subsection{Heston model, the COS method and its Greeks}
Under the risk-neutral measure the Heston model is
$\mathrm{d}S_t = rS_t\,\mathrm{d}t + \sqrt{v_t}S_t\,\mathrm{d}W_1$,
$\mathrm{d}v_t = \kappa(\theta-v_t)\,\mathrm{d}t + \xi\sqrt{v_t}\,\mathrm{d}W_2$,
$\mathrm{d}W_1\mathrm{d}W_2=\rho\,\mathrm{d}t$, with nine pricing inputs
$p=(S_0,K,T,r,v_0,\kappa,\theta,\xi,\rho)$. We use the characteristic function $\varphi$
of the log-price in the form of Albrecher et al.~\cite{albrecher}, which avoids the
complex-logarithm discontinuity. The COS method~\cite{fang} expands the density on a
truncation range $[a,b]$ in a cosine series; for a European put
\begin{equation}
V_{\mathrm{put}} \approx K e^{-rT}\,{\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}}{}^{\prime}\,
\mathrm{Re}\!\left[\varphi(u_k)\,e^{\,iu_k(x-a)}\right] U_k,\quad u_k=\tfrac{k\pi}{b-a},
\label{eq:cos}
\end{equation}
with $x=\ln(S_0/K)$, closed-form payoff coefficients $U_k$, and $\sum'$ halving the first
term. Calls follow from put--call parity. The $N$ terms are independent.

Delta and Gamma have closed-form COS expressions from the same sum~\cite[Remark~3.2]{fang},
analysed by Junike~\cite{junike}: since $x$ enters (\ref{eq:cos}) only through
$e^{iu_kx}$, $\partial/\partial S_0$ multiplies each term by $iu_k/S_0$, and
$\partial/\partial K$ follows from $x$ and the linearity of $U_k$ in $K$. These two
Greeks are therefore nearly free in any COS implementation. The derivatives with respect to
$T$, $r$ and the five model parameters ($v_0,\kappa,\theta,\xi,\rho$) enter through
$\varphi$, and these are where differentiation does real work.

\subsection{Computing Greeks}
Bump-and-reprice approximates $\partial V/\partial p_j$ by central differences: 19
pricings for nine Greeks, with truncation error $O(h^2)$ and rounding error $O(\epsilon/h)$.
Forward-mode AD carries one tangent per input direction. Reverse-mode (adjoint) AD
propagates $\bar v=\partial V/\partial v$ backwards from the output and returns all
derivatives in one sweep at a small constant multiple of the pricing cost~\cite{griewank};
software tools store the forward values on a tape, or recompute them under
checkpointing~\cite{griewank92}. For the Heston--Fourier price, Cui et al.~\cite{cui}
derived the gradient by hand.

\subsection{Hardware acceleration of option pricing}
FPGA work on option pricing covers Monte Carlo for the Heston model~\cite{deschryver,deschryver13},
binomial and trinomial trees~\cite{jin}, and multi-dimensional quadrature~\cite{tse}. For
Greeks, Klaisoongnoen et al.\ ported the STAC-A2 risk benchmark (Heston Monte Carlo with
Greeks by re-simulation, i.e.\ finite differences) to Alveo, Stratix-10 and
Versal devices~\cite{klai,klaih2rc,klai24}, and AMD's Vitis Quantitative Finance Library
computes Greeks by central differences~\cite{vitisquant}. The COS method has been
accelerated on GPUs, for multiple strikes and the Heston model~\cite{zhang09,zhang14}, and
adjoint AD runs on GPUs with a tape~\cite{gremse}. Table~\ref{tab:related} positions this
work; the reported figures measure different things and are not directly comparable. We
found no FPGA computation of Greeks by an adjoint and no FPGA COS implementation after
searching FPL, FCCM, FPT, ReConFig, TRETS, the HEART and H2RC workshops and general
indexes; absence from a search is not proof of absence.

\subsection{Precision analysis for FPGA datapaths}
Sizing fixed-point word lengths from sensitivities obtained by automatic differentiation
was introduced for FPGAs by Gaffar et al.~\cite{gaffar}, and accuracy-guaranteed
bit-width optimisation by affine arithmetic by Lee et al.~\cite{lee06}; Gappa~\cite{gappa}
gives machine-checked error enclosures. Our bound is Linnainmaa's first-order
analysis~\cite{linnainmaa}, applied to a datapath that contains its own adjoint operations,
so that it bounds the rounding error of each Greek as the hardware computes it. We do not
compare its tightness with an affine-arithmetic or Gappa enclosure.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}
\subsection{A register-resident tape}
A reverse sweep needs the forward values in reverse order; software AD stores them on a
tape. In (\ref{eq:cos}) the control flow does not depend on the input: every term runs the
same operations, the truncation range is fixed per evaluation, and there are no
data-dependent branches. The forward and reverse computations of one term therefore form a
fixed operation graph whose every value has a lifetime known at design time, and the
modulo scheduler allocates for each value the rotating registers that hold it across the
terms in flight. The tape is thus compiled into the schedule: it costs registers, not memory
traffic. Over all terms in flight, the 56-bit adjoint datapath needs 447 such registers,
against 150 for a price-only datapath of the same throughput and 374 for the factored
forward datapath; in Vivado the Greeks add 11,901 flip-flops and 1,129 shift-register LUTs
to the price-only engine (Section~\ref{sec:res-cost}).

The Greeks returned are the derivatives of the COS approximation, including the put--call
parity terms, with $[a,b]$ held fixed (A4 in Table~\ref{tab:assumptions}). Per term the
reverse sweep propagates adjoints from the term's contribution back to the model inputs,
reusing the forward pass's reciprocals; a power-of-two normalisation keeps the small
adjoints of high-frequency terms at full precision; nine accumulators sum the price and the
Greek contributions.

\subsection{Arithmetic}
The datapath has no dividers. Reciprocals and square roots use Newton--Raphson iterations
from table seeds, the exponential Tang's method~\cite{tang}, the logarithm a table and a
short series, and sines, cosines and complex magnitudes and angles CORDIC~\cite{volder}.
Each COS term costs 250 multiplications, three CORDIC rotations and two CORDIC vectorings
(126 multiplications for the price alone). Values are two's-complement fixed point with
$FL$ fraction bits (56-bit words with $FL=28$, or 64 with $FL=32$), rounded to nearest after
every operation. Data-dependent shifts are built only as wide as the ranges measured over
the input domain with margin, which cut logic by 35\%; an input that would exceed a range
raises a \texttt{range\_err} flag rather than return a wrong value.

\subsection{First-order rounding-error bound}
\label{sec:bound}
Let $\delta_i$ be the local error introduced at operation $i$, with $|\delta_i|\le\epsilon_i$.
To first order the error in an output $y$ is $\sum_i (\partial y/\partial w_i)\,\delta_i$,
where $w_i$ is the result of operation $i$, so
\begin{equation}
|\hat y - y| \;\le\; \sum_i \left|\frac{\partial y}{\partial w_i}\right| \epsilon_i
\;=:\; B_y ,
\label{eq:bound}
\end{equation}
Linnainmaa's first-order analysis~\cite{linnainmaa}. The sensitivities are obtained by a
reverse sweep over the operation graph, which includes the hardware's own adjoint
operations, so $B_y$ bounds the rounding error of each Greek as the hardware computes it.
Products of local errors are dropped; they are about $2^{-FL}$ times smaller
(Section~\ref{sec:res-acc}).

\textbf{What the bound covers.} $B_y$ bounds the difference between the fixed-point
datapath and the \emph{same graph} evaluated in exact arithmetic. The primitives are
expanded into elementary operations in the graph (the Newton--Raphson iterations, table
lookups and series terms), so every internal rounding has its own
$\epsilon_i = 2^{-(FL+1)}$; their approximation error (finite iterations, table resolution,
series truncation) is common to the fixed-point and exact evaluations of the graph and is
measured separately: at most $1.1\times10^{-8}$ relative, against an exact-function
reference, on the 21-case grid of Section~\ref{sec:res-acc}. Each CORDIC unit is one
operation whose local error was measured against its exact integer model over 30,000
inputs: rotation outputs are within 1.14\,ulp, modelled as $\epsilon = 2$\,ulp;
vectoring magnitudes within 0.8\,ulp; the vectoring angle loses precision as the input
magnitude falls (at most $1.75/|z|$\,ulp), and is modelled as
$\epsilon = 2\max(1, 1/|z|)$\,ulp at the evaluation point. The bound excludes the COS
truncation error (Section~\ref{sec:res-method}).

\textbf{How it is used.} $B_y$ is computed in software from the graph in 0.1\,s per
parameter set, so the engine does not attach it to each result. It serves at design time
to choose the word length and to certify, over a sampled input domain, the accuracy the
engine delivers (Table~\ref{tab:apriori}).

\subsection{Generator and modulo scheduling}
Fig.~\ref{fig:flow} shows the flow. One Python description of setup (once per evaluation),
term ($N=128$ times) and finish (once) is an operation graph from which the generator
derives the bit-accurate model, a double-precision model, the bound (\ref{eq:bound}) and a
modulo schedule~\cite{rau} emitted as Verilog. The testbench compares every register at the
cycle it becomes valid against the model (999 to 2,004 checks per design), so every
property shown for the model holds for the hardware. The terms are independent, so a new
term starts every initiation interval (II) while earlier ones are in flight
(Fig.~\ref{fig:sched}). With 8 multipliers, 3 CORDIC rotators and 2 vectorers, all three
resource classes give $\mathrm{II}=\lceil 250/8\rceil = 3{\cdot}32/3 = 2{\cdot}32/2 = 32$
cycles, and the loop takes $127\cdot 32 + 506 + 2 = 4{,}572$ cycles. The CORDIC units set
the pace, so the reverse sweep's 124 extra multiplications per term fit into multiplier
slots the forward pass leaves idle --- but only because the price-and-Greeks engine has
twice the multipliers a price-only engine needs for the same II: the price alone needs
$\lceil 126/32\rceil = 4$.

\section{Implementation}
\label{sec:impl}

host-setup configuration the setup and finish steps (about 100 multiplications and a few
transcendental functions per evaluation) run on the host as generated C that is bit-exact
with the model, and the FPGA runs the 128-term loop; the 64-bit configuration computes
everything on chip. Each engine sits behind an AXI4-Lite register file, driven for testing by
a JTAG-to-AXI master with no processor software. Table~\ref{tab:impl} gives the board
implementations.

\section{Experimental Methodology}
\label{sec:methodology}
\textbf{Input sets.} (i) A 21-case grid over moneyness, maturity, vol-of-vol and
correlation, calls and puts. (ii) A 10,500-input sweep: 6,000 uniform over the verified
domain ($S_0{=}100$, $K\in[60,150]$, $T\in[0.1,3]$, $r\in[0,0.1]$, $v_0,\theta\in[0.005,0.25]$,
$\kappa\in[0.2,6]$, $\xi\in[0.1,1]$, $\rho\in[-0.95,0.6]$), 500 in each of seven hard regimes
(Feller condition violated, $\rho$ near its limits, deep strikes, very short and long
maturities, high vol-of-vol, very low variance) and 1,000 with one input outside the
domain. (iii) A held-out sweep of the same composition with a fresh seed, drawn after the
shift ranges were fitted. (iv) 50 board cases (2 fixed, 48 random). (v) 50 calibrations of
the model to NIFTY index options on 50 trading days.

\textbf{References.} Rounding error is measured against the same graph in double precision;
the COS result is compared separately with an independent Fourier-integral price (method
error).

\textbf{Equal-throughput engines.} Three host-setup engines with II\,$=$\,32 were generated,
checked bit-exact against their models by the same testbench generator, and implemented out
of context in Vivado 2025.2 on the xc7z020clg484-1 at 70\,MHz: price only (4 multipliers),
adjoint (8) and factored forward mode (10). The forward datapath carries its tangents without
the per-term rescaling the adjoint applies to its adjoints, as a forward-mode AD tool would;
this leaves it less accurate, not larger.

\textbf{CPU baselines.} The identical algorithm in C++ (double precision, g++~13) on an Intel
Core i5-1155G7 laptop (4 cores, 8 threads): price only, bump-and-reprice (19 pricings), an
optimised bump that reuses the characteristic function, forward-mode AD (9 directions in one
pass), taped adjoint AD with CoDiPack~\cite{codipack}, and hand-derived analytic Greeks after
Cui et al.~\cite{cui}, all with \texttt{-O3 -march=native}. In that build the compiler leaves
the 128-term loop scalar. Two vectorised variants of the analytic method, built with
\texttt{-Ofast -fopenmp-simd} and glibc's vector math library, compute across terms: the
single loop made branch-free, which vectorises but spills its live complex temporaries and runs
slower than the scalar code, and the same work split into seven short loops over arrays of the
128 terms (structure of arrays), which vectorises cleanly and is the vectorised baseline
reported. Every method matches the reference model (adjoint to $1.3\times10^{-10}$ relative; the
vectorised variants agree with forward mode to $9.3\times10^{-12}$). Latency is the best of five passes over 2,000 inputs on one core of an
idle machine; throughput uses eight independent processes.

\subsection{Adjoint against forward mode}
\label{sec:res-modes}
Three forward-mode (tangent) datapaths were built by the same generator, with the same
primitives, range and scheduler: \emph{dense} (all eight directions through every operation,
as a vector forward-mode AD tool does; a weak baseline, shown for completeness),
\emph{sparse} (structural zeros skipped) and \emph{factored} (derivatives of the exponent with
respect to its inner complex quantities formed once per term, as a hand derivation of
analytic Greeks does). The factored one is the competitor that matters. All return the same
price and Greeks as the adjoint to $6\times10^{-14}$ in double precision. Per term the adjoint
needs 250 multiplications, the factored forward datapath 313 (Fig.~\ref{fig:modes},
Table~\ref{tab:modes}). With 8 multipliers this is 4,731 against 5,622 cycles; with 16, all but
the dense datapath are CORDIC-bound, and the factored one is slightly faster (4,571 against
4,619). At equal throughput (Table~\ref{tab:equal}) the factored forward engine is 2.7\% faster
in cycles but needs 6.8\% more LUTs, 12.2\% more flip-flops and 25\% more DSP blocks, and at
the same word length it is 6$\times$ less accurate (worst Greek error $7.4\times10^{-5}$ against
$1.2\times10^{-5}$), because its tangents are not rescaled per term. The adjoint's advantage over
a well-factored forward datapath is therefore real but modest: about a fifth of the
multipliers, a few percent of the logic, and accuracy.

\subsection{Rounding accuracy and the bound}
\label{sec:res-acc}
The bound covers rounding only; Section~\ref{sec:res-method} shows that the COS method's own
error can be far larger. Fig.~\ref{fig:accuracy} gives, on the 21-case grid, the worst
relative rounding error of each output against the same graph in double precision, and the
worst error as a fraction of its bound. On the 56-bit design the price is accurate to
$6.1\times10^{-7}$ and the Greeks to at most $1.2\times10^{-5}$; the 64-bit design gains one
to two orders of magnitude; no output reaches more than 0.103 of its bound.

\label{fig:accuracy}

The grid understates the tail. Over the 10,500-input sweep no unflagged result exceeded its
bound (worst 0.378) and there was no overflow; on the held-out sweep (Fig.~\ref{fig:boundsweep})
no in-domain input raised \texttt{range\_err}, the worst in-domain ratio was 0.356, and every

assumption (A6): evaluated in 50-digit arithmetic on 100 held-out inputs, the hardware error
is at most 0.24 of the bound; and the bound still holds at 44 bits, where second-order terms
are 4,096$\times$ larger. Table~\ref{tab:sigfig} gives the significant figures delivered on the
held-out inputs, and Table~\ref{tab:apriori} the bound itself, relative to each value, as an
a-priori accuracy guide over the sampled domain. The price and the market Greeks keep at least
5.3 figures for 99\% of inputs; $\partial V/\partial\kappa$ and $\partial V/\partial\xi$, small
and computed through long chains with cancellation, keep 3.5 and 3.0 at the 99th percentile and
only 2.2 and 1.4 at worst, and for $\partial V/\partial\xi$ the bound can exceed the value.

\subsection{COS method error}
\label{sec:res-method}
On the 50 board cases the COS result was compared with an independent Fourier-integral price.
The method error has a median of $2\times10^{-11}$ for the price, but in a few in-domain cases
it far exceeds the bounded rounding error: for vega it reaches $7.8\times10^{-3}$ at $\xi=1$,
$T=0.34$, where 128 terms are too few (256 remove it), and a deep out-of-the-money put needs a
1.5$\times$ wider range. The engine does not flag these cases. The total error against the Heston
model is at most the rounding bound plus the method error, and only the former is bounded;
any implementation of this COS configuration shares the method error. A deployment would
restrict the domain where 128 terms suffice or use $N=256$, which roughly doubles the cycles.

\subsection{Against a CPU: latency, throughput and energy}
\label{sec:res-cpu}
Fig.~\ref{fig:latency} and Table~\ref{tab:cpu} compare the same algorithm on the FPGA and on
one laptop core. The FPGA figures are computed engine latencies (A9): they exclude the bus
transfers (39 register accesses) and, for the ZedBoard, the host's setup and finish, while the
CPU figures are measured whole evaluations. Against the general-purpose methods the ZCU102 engine
is faster: 4.9$\times$ than forward-mode AD, 6.5$\times$ than taped adjoint AD and 15$\times$ than
bump-and-reprice. Against hand-derived analytic Greeks it is 1.3$\times$ faster than the scalar
build (20.3\,$\mu$s) but slower than the vectorised one: at \SIMDUS\,$\mu$s, one vectorised
core is 1.17$\times$ faster than the engine's computed latency, before the engine's omitted
transfers are counted; the ZedBoard's loop is 3.2$\times$ slower than the scalar build. We therefore claim
no latency advantage over a well-optimised CPU implementation. With eight processes on the four
cores the vectorised build reaches \SIMDTP\ evaluations per second (the scalar build 144,000 in the
same session); one 64-bit engine gives 62,800, and the three engines that would fit on the ZCU102 by
LUTs (a projection, not built) about 188,000 per second, so the CPU also leads in throughput.
Throughput on the laptop varies by about $\pm$25\% with its temperature, so the figures used for
energy are those measured in the same run as the power.

\section{Discussion and Limitations}
\label{sec:discussion}
\textbf{What the results support.} For a pricer without data-dependent control flow, a
hand-derived reverse sweep can share one statically scheduled datapath with the forward pass,
with its tape in scheduled registers. At equal throughput this costs about 1.7$\times$ the area of
a price-only engine at 1.03$\times$ its cycles, and it is cheaper in multipliers and logic, and
more accurate at the same word length, than a well-factored forward-mode datapath built by the
same flow. Each Greek has a first-order rounding bound that held on every input tested and on
silicon. These are the claims; speed and energy against a CPU are not: a vectorised laptop core is
faster, four cores give more throughput, and the energy per evaluation is the same at chip level.

\textbf{What they do not.} The adjoint's advantage over a factored forward datapath is modest
(Section~\ref{sec:res-modes}). Delta and the strike sensitivity are nearly free in any COS
implementation, so the adjoint's work is in the seven Greeks that enter through $\varphi$. The
bound does not cover the COS method error, which dominates in a few in-domain cases, and it is a
design-time tool, not attached to each result. Against a vectorised CPU implementation of
hand-derived Greeks the engine is not faster, and its latency was computed, not measured.
Gamma, the most-used second-order Greek, is not computed (A12).

\textbf{Strike chains.} All strikes of one expiry share the COS grid, so one characteristic
function and one reverse sweep per term can serve every strike, each extra strike costing about
7\% of one option; this was scheduled and emulated (32 strikes in 4,892 cycles on the 64-bit
design) but not built, and the same sharing helps a CPU (2.3\,$\mu$s per strike at 32 strikes on
one core).

\textbf{Further limitations.} The Zynq-7020 design closes timing at 79\,MHz, not 100\,MHz; the
input domain is bounded (A1); and end-to-end latency over the processor bus and board-level power
have not been measured.

% =============================================================================
\section{Conclusion}
For the COS method, whose operations do not depend on the input, a hand-derived reverse sweep can
be co-scheduled with the forward pass in one fixed-point datapath, with its tape held in
statically allocated registers. Measured against price-only and forward-mode engines of equal
throughput built by the same flow, the nine Greeks cost 1.03$\times$ the cycles and about
1.7$\times$ the area of the price, with fewer resources and better accuracy than the forward-mode
alternative; each Greek carries a first-order rounding bound that was never exceeded, and the
engine ran bit-exact on two FPGA families, including on real market calibrations. Deriving the
reverse sweep automatically, adding Gamma, measuring end-to-end latency and board power, and
placing several engines per device are the next steps.
