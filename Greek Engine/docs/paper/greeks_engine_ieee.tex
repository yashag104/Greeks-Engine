\title{Tape-Free Adjoint Greeks on FPGAs: Heston-Model\\ Option Sensitivities with Per-Greek Rounding-Error Bounds}

\author{\IEEEauthorblockN{Yash Agrawal}
\IEEEauthorblockA{Birla Institute of Technology and Science, Pilani\\ Pilani, India}
\and
\IEEEauthorblockN{Govind Prasad}
\IEEEauthorblockA{Birla Institute of Technology and Science, Pilani\\ Pilani, India}}

\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Option Greeks, the sensitivities of a price to its inputs, drive hedging and risk
limits, and on hardware they are still computed by bump-and-reprice: one extra pricing
per input, at an accuracy that depends on an arbitrary bump size. This paper presents
the first FPGA engine that computes Greeks by adjoint (reverse-mode) differentiation.
It prices a European option under the Heston stochastic-volatility model with the COS
Fourier-cosine method and returns the price and all nine first-order Greeks in one pass.
Because the COS pricer performs the same operations for every input, its reverse sweep
is compiled into the same statically scheduled fixed-point datapath as the forward
pass, with no tape memory or replay logic, and fills multiplier slots the forward pass
leaves idle. A generator produces, from one operation graph, a bit-accurate model, a
first-order rounding-error bound for the price and each Greek, a modulo-scheduled
Verilog datapath and the host code. On a Zynq-7020 the price and nine Greeks take
4,733 cycles, 1.04 times the price alone and 18.4 times fewer than bump-and-reprice on
the same hardware; the adjoint Greeks are 32--560 times more accurate than the best
bump-and-reprice. The bound was never exceeded over 21,000 test inputs (worst 0.378 of
the bound), and the engine ran bit-exact on silicon on two FPGA families: 450/450
results on a ZedBoard and 1,010/1,010 on a Zynq UltraScale+ ZCU102, including 50
calibrations to real NIFTY option data. On the ZCU102 an evaluation takes 15.9\,$\mu$s,
4.9--15 times faster than forward-mode AD, taped adjoint AD and bump-and-reprice on a
laptop core and 1.3 times faster than hand-derived analytic Greeks.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
A trading desk needs not only the prices of the options it holds but their Greeks,
the partial derivatives of each price with respect to spot, strike, maturity, rate and
the model's parameters. Greeks set hedge ratios and risk limits and feed model
calibration, so they are recomputed whenever the market moves. Under the Heston
stochastic-volatility model~\cite{heston} the price has no closed form and is computed
numerically, here by the COS method~\cite{fang}.

The usual way to obtain Greeks, in software and in every FPGA design we know of, is
\emph{bump-and-reprice}: perturb one input and price again. Nine Greeks by central
differences cost 19 pricings, and the result depends on a bump size for which no
setting is safe for every Greek (Fig.~\ref{fig:bump}). Software replaced this with
\emph{adjoint algorithmic differentiation} (AAD)~\cite{giles,capriotti}, which obtains
the derivatives of one output with respect to all inputs in a single reverse sweep. In
software, however, the reverse sweep reads a \emph{tape} of the forward computation;
on our CPU baseline taped AAD costs 7.6 pricings and forward-mode AD 5.7. No FPGA work
has computed Greeks by an adjoint~\cite{klai,deschryver,vitisquant,capgiles24}.

This paper shows that, for a Fourier pricer, the adjoint maps onto hardware unusually
well. The COS method performs exactly the same operations for every input, so the
lifetime of every forward value is known at design time and the reverse sweep can be
scheduled statically, alongside the forward pass, on shared arithmetic units. The
contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{A tape-free adjoint datapath} for Heston--COS Greeks: the reverse sweep
is compiled into the forward pass's fixed-point datapath with no tape memory or replay
logic (Section~\ref{sec:method}). To our knowledge this is the first FPGA computation of
option Greeks by adjoint differentiation, and the first FPGA implementation of the COS
method.
\item \textbf{All nine Greeks for 1.04 times the cost of the price}: the reverse sweep
fills multiplier slots left idle by the CORDIC-bound forward pass. Against forward-mode
datapaths built by the same generator, the adjoint needs the fewest multipliers for the
same throughput (Section~\ref{sec:res-modes}).
\item \textbf{A first-order rounding-error bound for every Greek} of a fixed-point
datapath that itself performs the adjoint, computed from the same operation graph as
the hardware and never exceeded over 21,000 inputs and on silicon
(Section~\ref{sec:res-acc}).
\item \textbf{A generator} that emits, from one description, the bit-accurate model,
the bound, the modulo schedule, the Verilog, the bus wrappers and the host C code, and
\textbf{bit-exact silicon results on two FPGA families} (Section~\ref{sec:res-silicon}).
\end{enumerate}
We state plainly what is not claimed: the reverse sweep is derived by hand in the
generator's graph, not by an AD tool; the bound is first order and covers rounding
only; and on the small FPGA a CPU core with hand-derived analytic Greeks is faster
(Section~\ref{sec:discussion}).

\section{Background and Related Work}
\label{sec:background}
\subsection{Heston model and the COS method}
Under the risk-neutral measure the Heston model is
$\mathrm{d}S_t = rS_t\,\mathrm{d}t + \sqrt{v_t}S_t\,\mathrm{d}W_1$,
$\mathrm{d}v_t = \kappa(\theta-v_t)\,\mathrm{d}t + \xi\sqrt{v_t}\,\mathrm{d}W_2$,
$\mathrm{d}W_1\mathrm{d}W_2=\rho\,\mathrm{d}t$, with nine pricing inputs
$p=(S_0,K,T,r,v_0,\kappa,\theta,\xi,\rho)$. The characteristic function $\varphi$ of
the log-price is known in closed form; we use the form of Albrecher
et al.~\cite{albrecher}, which avoids the complex-logarithm discontinuity. The COS
method~\cite{fang} expands the density on a truncation range $[a,b]$ in a cosine
series, giving for a European put
\begin{equation}
V_{\mathrm{put}} \approx K e^{-rT}\,{\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}}{}^{\prime}\,
\mathrm{Re}\!\left[\varphi(u_k)\,e^{\,iu_k(x-a)}\right] U_k,\quad u_k=\tfrac{k\pi}{b-a},
\label{eq:cos}
\end{equation}
with $x=\ln(S_0/K)$, closed-form payoff coefficients $U_k$, and $\sum'$ halving the
first term. Calls follow from put--call parity, $V_{\mathrm{call}} = V_{\mathrm{put}} +
S_0 - Ke^{-rT}$. The $N$ terms are \emph{independent}, which the hardware pipelines.

\subsection{Computing Greeks}
\emph{Bump-and-reprice} approximates $\partial V/\partial p_j$ by
$[V(p+h e_j)-V(p-h e_j)]/2h$: 19 pricings for nine Greeks, with a truncation error
$O(h^2)$ and a rounding error $O(\epsilon/h)$, so the error is U-shaped in $h$.
\emph{Forward-mode} AD carries a tangent per input direction. \emph{Reverse-mode} (adjoint)
AD propagates $\bar v=\partial V/\partial v$ backwards from the output and returns all
nine derivatives in one sweep at a small constant multiple of the pricing
cost~\cite{griewank}; it is standard for Greeks in software~\cite{giles,capriotti}. For
the Heston--Fourier price, Cui et al.~\cite{cui} derived the gradient by hand.

\subsection{Hardware option pricing}
Published FPGA finance work is mostly Monte Carlo pricing reported as batch runtime or
energy against multi-core servers. Table~\ref{tab:related} positions this work; the
reported figures measure different things and are not directly comparable. None of the
hardware works computes Greeks by an adjoint, none uses a Fourier method, and none gives
a per-Greek accuracy guarantee for its fixed-point arithmetic.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}
\subsection{Why the adjoint needs no tape here}
In software, a reverse sweep needs the forward values in reverse order, which is why
AAD tools record a tape. In (\ref{eq:cos}) the control flow does not depend on the
input: every term runs the same operations, the truncation range is fixed per
evaluation, and there are no data-dependent branches. The forward and reverse
computations of one term therefore form a fixed operation graph whose every value has a
lifetime known at design time. The scheduler keeps each forward value in a register for
exactly as long as the reverse sweep needs it (447 storage registers per term in the
Zynq-7020 design), so the ``tape'' is compiled into the schedule and costs no memory
traffic.

The Greeks returned are the derivatives of the COS approximation itself, including the
put--call parity terms, with $[a,b]$ held fixed (Section~\ref{sec:assumptions}). Per term the
reverse sweep propagates adjoints from the term's contribution back to the eight model
inputs, reusing the forward pass's reciprocals; a power-of-two normalisation keeps the
small adjoints of high-frequency terms at full precision; and nine accumulators sum the
price and the Greek contributions.

\subsection{Arithmetic and fixed point}
The datapath has no dividers. Reciprocals and square roots use Newton--Raphson
iterations from table seeds, the exponential uses Tang's method~\cite{tang}, the
logarithm a table and a short series, and sines, cosines and complex magnitudes and
angles CORDIC~\cite{volder}. Each COS term costs exactly 250 multiplications, three
CORDIC rotations and two CORDIC vectorings (126 multiplications for the price alone).
Values are two's-complement fixed point with $FL$ fraction bits (56-bit words with
$FL=28$ on the Zynq-7020, 64-bit with $FL=32$ on UltraScale+), rounded to nearest after
every operation. Data-dependent shifts are built only as wide as the ranges measured
over the input domain with margin, which cut logic by 35\%; an input that would exceed
a measured range raises a \texttt{range\_err} flag rather than return a wrong value.

\subsection{First-order rounding-error bound}
Let $\delta_i$ be the rounding error introduced at operation $i$, with
$|\delta_i|\le 2^{-(FL+1)}$ for round-to-nearest. To first order the error in an output
$y$ is $\sum_i (\partial y/\partial w_i)\,\delta_i$, where $w_i$ is the result of
operation $i$, so
\begin{equation}
|\hat y - y| \;\le\; \sum_i \left|\frac{\partial y}{\partial w_i}\right| 2^{-(FL+1)}
\;=:\; B_y ,
\label{eq:bound}
\end{equation}
Linnainmaa's first-order analysis~\cite{linnainmaa}. The sensitivities
$\partial y/\partial w_i$ are themselves obtained by a reverse sweep over the operation
graph, which here includes the hardware's own adjoint operations, so $B_y$ bounds the
rounding error of each Greek as the hardware computes it. $B_y$ is computed in software
from the same graph (0.1\,s per parameter set); the engine does not output it. Products
of rounding errors are dropped; they are about $2^{-FL}$ times smaller, and two checks in
Section~\ref{sec:res-acc} confirm this.

\subsection{The generator and modulo scheduling}
Fig.~\ref{fig:flow} shows the flow. One Python description of setup (once per
evaluation), term ($N=128$ times) and finish (once) is an operation graph from which the
generator derives the bit-accurate fixed-point model, a double-precision model, the
bound (\ref{eq:bound}), and a modulo schedule~\cite{rau} that is emitted as Verilog.
Because the RTL and the model come from the same graph, the testbench compares every
register at the cycle it becomes valid (1,213--1,962 checks per design), and every
property shown for the model holds for the hardware.

The terms are independent, so a new term starts every initiation interval (II) while
earlier terms are in flight (Fig.~\ref{fig:sched}). With 8 shared multipliers, 3 CORDIC
rotators and 2 vectorers, all three resource classes give the same interval,
$\lceil 250/8\rceil = 3{\cdot}32/3 = 2{\cdot}32/2 = 32$ cycles, and an evaluation takes
$127\cdot 32 + 506 + 2 = 4{,}572$ cycles for the loop. Because the CORDIC units set the
pace, the forward pass leaves multiplier slots idle in every 32-cycle window; the reverse
sweep's 124 extra multiplications fill them, which is why the Greeks cost 4\%.

\section{Experimental Methodology}
\label{sec:methodology}
\textbf{Input sets.} (i) A 21-case grid over moneyness, maturity, vol-of-vol and
correlation, calls and puts. (ii) A 10,500-input sweep: 6,000 uniform over the verified
domain ($S_0{=}100$, $K\in[60,150]$, $T\in[0.1,3]$, $r\in[0,0.1]$, $v_0,\theta\in[0.005,0.25]$,
$\kappa\in[0.2,6]$, $\xi\in[0.1,1]$, $\rho\in[-0.95,0.6]$), 500 in each of seven hard
regimes (Feller condition violated, $\rho$ near its limits, deep strikes, very short and
long maturities, high vol-of-vol, very low variance) and 1,000 with one input outside the
domain. (iii) A held-out sweep of the same composition with a fresh seed. (iv) 50 board
cases (2 fixed, 48 random). (v) 50 calibrations of the model to NIFTY index option
quotes on 50 trading days.

\textbf{References.} Accuracy is measured against the same algorithm in double
precision (rounding error) and, separately, the COS result against an independent
Fourier-integral price (method error).

\textbf{CPU baselines.} The identical algorithm in C++ (double precision, g++~13,
\texttt{-O3 -march=native}) on an Intel Core i5-1155G7 laptop (4 cores, 8 threads):
price only, bump-and-reprice (19 pricings), an optimised bump that reuses the
characteristic function, forward-mode AD (9 directions in one pass), taped adjoint AD
with CoDiPack~\cite{codipack}, and hand-derived analytic Greeks after Cui et
al.~\cite{cui}. Every method matches the reference model (adjoint to
$1.3\times10^{-10}$ relative). Latency is the best of five passes over 2,000 inputs on
one core of an otherwise idle machine; throughput uses eight independent processes.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}
\subsection{Cost of the Greeks}
\label{sec:res-cycles}
Fig.~\ref{fig:cycles} compares, on identical hardware, the price alone, the price with
nine adjoint Greeks, and bump-and-reprice. On the 56-bit design the nine Greeks add 165
cycles (3.6\%) to the price; bump-and-reprice needs 18.4$\times$ more cycles. On the 64-bit
design, where the 32 multipliers rather than the CORDIC units set the pace, the Greeks
cost 1.55 pricings and bump-and-reprice 12.3$\times$ the adjoint. Against our own
first-generation, hand-written engine (433,779 cycles, which did not fit a Zynq-7020) the
generated design is 92$\times$ faster.

\subsection{Adjoint against forward mode}
\label{sec:res-modes}
Three forward-mode (tangent) datapaths were built by the same generator, with the same
primitives, range and scheduler: \emph{dense} (all eight directions through every
operation, as a vector forward-mode AD tool does), \emph{sparse} (structural zeros
skipped) and \emph{factored} (derivatives of the exponent with respect to its inner
complex quantities formed once per term, as a hand derivation of analytic Greeks does).
All return the same price and Greeks as the adjoint to $6\times10^{-14}$ in double
precision. Fig.~\ref{fig:modes} and Table~\ref{tab:modes} show that the adjoint needs the
fewest multiplications: 124 per term beyond the price, against 187 for the best
forward method. The 1.04$\times$ cost is not unique to the adjoint: with 16 multipliers all
but the dense datapath become CORDIC-bound at about 4,570 cycles. What the adjoint saves is
hardware: it reaches that pace with 8 multipliers (72 DSP blocks), where the factored
forward datapath needs 10 (90) and the dense one 26 (234, more than the device has).

\subsection{Accuracy and the error bound}
\label{sec:res-acc}
Fig.~\ref{fig:accuracy} gives the worst relative error of each output over the 21-case
grid against the same algorithm in double precision, and the worst error as a fraction
of its bound (\ref{eq:bound}). The 56-bit price is accurate to $6.1\times10^{-7}$ and the
Greeks to at most $1.2\times10^{-5}$; the 64-bit design gains one to two orders of
magnitude. No output reaches more than 0.103 of its bound.

\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/fig_accuracy.pdf}
\caption{Accuracy over the 21-case grid. (a) Worst relative error of the price $V$ and each Greek against the same algorithm in double precision. (b) Worst measured error divided by the first-order bound (\ref{eq:bound}); 1 would mean the bound is met with equality.}
\label{fig:accuracy}

Over the 10,500-input sweep no unflagged result exceeded its bound (worst 0.378), and
there was no overflow. On the held-out sweep (Fig.~\ref{fig:boundsweep}) none of the
9,500 in-domain inputs raised \texttt{range\_err}, the worst in-domain ratio was 0.356,

the hardware error is at most 0.24 of the bound, the same to three decimals as against
double precision; and the bound still holds at 44 bits, where second-order terms are
4,096$\times$ larger. Table~\ref{tab:sigfig} gives the significant figures delivered: the
price and the market Greeks keep at least 5.3 figures for 99\% of inputs, while
$\partial V/\partial\kappa$ and $\partial V/\partial\xi$, small and computed through long
chains with cancellation, keep 3.5 and 3.0. Each comes with its bound, so a user knows
its accuracy before using it.

\subsection{COS method error}
\label{sec:res-method}
The bound covers rounding only. On the 50 board cases the COS result was compared with an
independent Fourier-integral price: the method error has a median of $2\times10^{-11}$ for
the price but reaches $7.8\times10^{-3}$ for vega in the worst case ($\xi=1$, $T=0.34$),
where 128 terms are too few (256 terms remove it), and a deep out-of-the-money put needs a
1.5$\times$ wider range. The total error against the Heston model is at most the rounding
bound plus this method error, and only the former is bounded. Any implementation of this
COS configuration, in hardware or software, shares the method error.

\section{Discussion and Limitations}
\label{sec:discussion}
\textbf{Where the advantage lies.} The engine's case against a CPU is the cost and the
certified accuracy of its Greeks, not raw speed: the nine Greeks cost 4\% over the price in
hardware against 50\% for the best hand-written software and 470--660\% for AD tools, each
Greek carries a bound, and the latency is deterministic. The latency lead over hand-derived
Greeks is narrow (1.3$\times$) and exists only on the larger device.

\textbf{Strike chains.} All strikes of one expiry share the COS grid, so one
characteristic function and one reverse sweep per term can serve every strike, with each
extra strike costing about 7\% of one option. This was scheduled and emulated
(32 strikes in 4,892 cycles on the 64-bit design) but not built, and the same sharing
helps a CPU (2.3\,$\mu$s per strike at 32 strikes on one core), so it is not claimed as an
advantage.

\textbf{Limitations.} (i) The reverse sweep is derived by hand (A13); generating it
automatically from the forward graph is the natural next step. (ii) The bound is first
order and covers rounding only (A6, Section~\ref{sec:res-method}). (iii) The FPGA energy is
from simulated activity of the engine alone (A10), and the bus transfers are not timed
(A9); a processor-based ZCU102 design that times every stage and reads the board's power
monitors has been built but not yet run successfully. (iv) The Zynq-7020 design closes
timing at 79\,MHz, not 100\,MHz. (v) The input domain is bounded (A1).

% =============================================================================
\section{Conclusion}
For the COS method, whose operations do not depend on the input, adjoint differentiation
needs no tape: the reverse sweep can be compiled into the forward pass's datapath and fill
the multiplier slots the forward pass leaves idle. The resulting FPGA engine returns a
Heston price and all nine Greeks for 1.04 times the cost of the price, 18.4$\times$ fewer
cycles and 32--560$\times$ more accurate than bump-and-reprice on the same hardware, with a
first-order rounding-error bound on every Greek that was never exceeded, and it ran
bit-exact on two FPGA families, including on real market calibrations. Generating the
reverse sweep automatically and placing several engines per device are the next steps.
