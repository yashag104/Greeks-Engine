GREEKS ENGINE / FULL COURSE

Option Greeks in one hardware pass

Everything in this project, from what an option is to why a modulo reservation table is the reason all nine Greeks cost 1.04 pricings. Written as a course: each section assumes only the ones before it. The last sections are the paper.

00Map of the whole thing

Before any detail, here is the entire project in one paragraph, so every later section has somewhere to attach.

A bank holds a portfolio of options. To manage its risk it needs, continuously, not just the price of each option but the derivatives of that price with respect to every model parameter — the Greeks. Under the Heston stochastic-volatility model there is no closed-form price, so the price is computed by a numerical method (we use the Fourier-cosine, or COS, method). The standard way to get Greeks from a numerical pricer is bump-and-reprice: nudge each parameter, price again, take a difference. For n Greeks that costs n+1 pricings and it is numerically poor. The better way is reverse-mode algorithmic differentiation (AAD), which gets all n Greeks from one backward sweep over the pricing computation. AAD is standard in software. Nobody had built it as a hardware datapath. That is what this project is: a generator that takes the Heston-COS price and its adjoint sweep as a single operation graph and emits a fixed-point, resource-shared, modulo-scheduled Verilog datapath that produces the price and nine sensitivities in 4,731 clock cycles on a Zynq-7020-sized design — where bump-and-reprice on the identical hardware takes 86,803.

The product

A parameterised Verilog generator plus the RTL it emits, verified bit-exact against a golden model, with a per-Greek error bound.

The headline

All 9 Greeks for 1.04 pricings of extra work. In software, AAD typically costs 3–5× a pricing.

The honest gap

Everything is RTL simulation and Yosys estimates. No Vivado place-and-route has run yet, so there is no Fmax, no timing closure, no power, no bitstream.

The five things that had to be true at once

It is worth naming these early, because almost every design decision later is one of them being forced:

  1. The computation graph must be known at design time. Hardware cannot conveniently record a runtime tape. This is why the COS method was chosen over Monte Carlo — COS has a fixed term count and no data-dependent branching.
  2. There must be no dividers. Division and square root in fixed point are bit-serial and slow; in generation 1 they were 80 % of the latency. Everything must be reducible to multiplies.
  3. Arithmetic must be shared. A 56×56 multiplier costs real DSP slices. Inferring one per multiply operation put generation 1 at 159 multipliers, far past the 220 DSPs on a Zynq-7020.
  4. Fixed-point error must be bounded, not just measured. Adjoints accumulate through hundreds of operations; a paper needs a statement stronger than "we tried 21 cases".
  5. The comparison must be fair. Beating a CPU proves nothing. The baseline has to be bump-and-reprice on the same pricer, same word length, same arithmetic.

Part I — The finance

01Options, and why anyone wants derivatives of a price

A European call option on a stock gives its holder the right, but not the obligation, to buy one share at a fixed strike price K at a fixed future date T. If the share is worth ST at that date, the holder exercises only when it pays:

payoff_call = max(S_T − K, 0) payoff_put = max(K − S_T, 0)

The option's value today is the expected payoff, discounted, under a probability measure in which the stock drifts at the risk-free rate r (the risk-neutral measure):

V = e^(−rT) · E_Q[ payoff(S_T) ]

1.1 Black-Scholes, in one line of reasoning

If the stock follows geometric Brownian motion with constant volatility σ,

dS_t = r S_t dt + σ S_t dW_t

then this SDE has an explicit solution, ST is log-normal, and the expectation above can be done in closed form. That gives the Black-Scholes formula:

V_call = S_0 N(d1) − K e^(−rT) N(d2) d1 = [ ln(S_0/K) + (r + σ²/2) T ] / (σ √T) d2 = d1 − σ √T

where N is the standard normal CDF. Two facts matter for this project: the formula exists, and it is differentiable in closed form. That makes Black-Scholes the perfect first test case — you can check an AAD engine against exact answers.

1.2 The Greeks: what they are and why a desk needs them

A Greek is a partial derivative of the option value with respect to one input. They are not academic curiosities; they are the instructions for hedging.

GreekDefinitionWhat it tells a trader
Delta∂V/∂SHow many shares to hold to be immune to a small move in the stock. This is the hedge ratio.
Vega∂V/∂σ or ∂V/∂v₀Exposure to volatility itself changing. Cannot be hedged with stock — needs other options.
Rho∂V/∂rExposure to interest rates.
Theta∂V/∂TTime decay: what the position loses simply by a day passing.
Gamma∂²V/∂S²How fast Delta itself moves — how often the hedge must be rebalanced.

The economic point: a desk that is delta-hedged holds −∂V/∂S shares against each option, so that small stock moves cancel. Its remaining risk is in the other Greeks. Recomputing all of them, for a whole book, repeatedly through the trading day, is the computation that actually consumes bank compute.

Why this becomes a hardware problem. One option's Greeks are cheap. A book of hundreds of thousands of positions, revalued under hundreds of market scenarios, is not. The cost scales as (positions) × (scenarios) × (Greeks per position) × (cost of one pricing). AAD attacks the third factor, taking it from n to ~1. Hardware attacks the fourth. This project does both at once.

02The Heston model: why the closed form disappears

Black-Scholes assumes σ is a constant. Every property of real option markets says otherwise:

  • The volatility smile. Back out the implied σ from traded prices at different strikes and you get a curve, not a flat line. If BS were right it would be flat.
  • Volatility clusters. Turbulent days follow turbulent days.
  • Fat tails. Crashes happen far more often than a log-normal allows.
  • The leverage effect. When price falls, volatility rises.

Heston (1993) makes the variance itself a stochastic process — specifically a Cox-Ingersoll-Ross (CIR) square-root process — correlated with the stock:

dS_t = r S_t dt + √(v_t) S_t dW_t^S dv_t = κ(θ − v_t) dt + ξ √(v_t) dW_t^v dW^S · dW^v = ρ dt
ParamNameRoleTypical
v₀initial variancewhere volatility is today0.01–0.09
κmean-reversion speedhow fast v is pulled back to θ0.5–5
θlong-run variancethe level it is pulled toward0.01–0.09
ξvol-of-volhow noisy the variance process is0.1–1.0
ρcorrelationthe leverage effect; negative in equities−0.9…−0.3

The drift term κ(θ − vt) is what produces mean reversion: it is negative when v > θ and positive when v < θ. Negative ρ produces the skew, because a falling stock now comes with rising volatility. The Feller condition 2κθ > ξ² keeps v strictly positive; many calibrated real-world parameter sets violate it, which is one reason numerical robustness matters.

2.1 Why there is no formula, and what replaces it

Two things break. First, ST has no explicit solution, because you cannot integrate √(vt) without knowing the whole path of v. Second, the joint law of (ST, vT) is not a standard distribution.

What Heston did find in closed form is the characteristic function of the log price — the Fourier transform of its density:

φ(u) = E[ e^(i u ln S_T) ] = exp( C(u,T) + D(u,T)·v₀ + i u ln S₀ ) d(u) = √( (ρ ξ u i − κ)² + ξ²(u i + u²) ) g(u) = (κ − ρ ξ u i − d) / (κ − ρ ξ u i + d) C(u,T) = r u i T + (κθ/ξ²)[ (κ − ρξui − d)T − 2 ln( (1 − g e^(−dT)) / (1 − g) ) ] D(u,T) = ((κ − ρξui − d)/ξ²) · (1 − e^(−dT)) / (1 − g e^(−dT))

Read that as an algorithm, not a formula — it is a fixed sequence of complex arithmetic: one complex square root, two complex divisions, one complex exponential, one complex logarithm, and a handful of complex multiplies. That sequence is the thing we build in silicon. Everything from section 8 onward is about performing exactly these operations without dividers and with shared multipliers.

Consequence for Greeks. No closed-form price means no closed-form Greeks. Under Heston there are at least 7 parameter sensitivities (plus Delta and strike sensitivity), against Black-Scholes' 5 — so the n in "bump-and-reprice costs n+1 pricings" gets worse exactly where pricing also got harder. This is the pressure that makes AAD worth building.

Our engine emits ten outputs: the price plus nine sensitivities — delta (∂V/∂S₀), strike_sens (∂V/∂K), theta_greek (∂V/∂T), rho_greek (∂V/∂r), vega (∂V/∂v₀), kappa_sens, theta_sens, xi_sens, rho_corr (∂V/∂ρ).

Part II — The numerics

03The COS method: turning an integral into a fixed sum

This section is the closest analogue to "why radix-N FFT gives you a butterfly diagram". The COS method is a restructuring of Fourier inversion that replaces an oscillatory infinite integral with a short, fixed-length, exponentially-convergent sum of independent terms. That restructuring is what makes a hardware datapath possible at all.

3.1 The problem it solves

We have φ(u) and want the price. The textbook route is direct Fourier inversion:

V_call = S₀ − ½ K e^(−rT) + (K e^(−rT)/π) ∫₀^∞ Re[ e^(−iu ln K) φ(u) / (iu) ] du

This is bad for hardware in three separate ways: the integrand oscillates (so quadrature needs many points and adapts), the upper limit is infinite (so the point count is data-dependent), and the 1/(iu) has a singularity at the origin. All three break the "fixed computation known at design time" requirement.

MethodCostWhy not / why
Direct quadrature (Carr-Madan)adaptiveOscillatory integrand, data-dependent point count.
FFT over strikesO(N log N)Gives a whole grid of strikes at once — wasteful when you want one strike, and the butterfly network is a large fixed interconnect.
COS (Fang & Oosterlee, 2008)O(N), N ≈ 128Exponential convergence, fixed N, no RNG, every term independent. Chosen.

3.2 The derivation, in four moves

Move 1 — truncate. The density of the log-price has effectively finite support. Pick an interval [a,b] that contains essentially all the mass. Now we are on a finite domain, where Fourier series apply instead of Fourier transforms.

Move 2 — expand in cosines. On [a,b], any well-behaved density has a cosine series:

f(y) ≈ Σ'_{k=0}^{N−1} A_k · cos( kπ (y − a)/(b − a) ) A_k = (2/(b−a)) ∫_a^b f(y) cos( kπ (y−a)/(b−a) ) dy (Σ' halves the k = 0 term)

Move 3 — recognise the characteristic function. This is the trick. Write the cosine as the real part of a complex exponential, and the integral for Ak becomes exactly a characteristic-function evaluation:

A_k ≈ (2/(b−a)) · Re[ φ( kπ/(b−a) ) · exp( −i kπa/(b−a) ) ]

We never touch the density f. We go straight from φ, which Heston gave us in closed form, to the series coefficients. Define uk = kπ/(b−a) — the frequency grid.

Move 4 — integrate against the payoff analytically. The price is the inner product of the density with the payoff. Since the density is now a cosine series, and the payoff is max(e^y − 1, 0), that inner product can be done by hand, once, forever. Its coefficients Vk have a closed form. The price collapses to a sum of products:

V = e^(−rT) · Σ'_{k=0}^{N−1} Re[ φ(u_k) · e^(−i u_k a) ] · V_k

That is the COS method. An integral became a 128-term sum of independent products, each of which requires one characteristic-function evaluation.

3.3 The payoff coefficients

For a call, with the payoff nonzero on [0,b]:

V_k = (2/(b−a)) · K · [ χ_k(0,b) − ψ_k(0,b) ] χ_k(c,d) = 1/(1 + u_k²) · [ e^d cos(u_k(d−a)) − e^c cos(u_k(c−a)) + u_k ( e^d sin(u_k(d−a)) − e^c sin(u_k(c−a)) ) ] ψ_k(c,d) = (b−a)/(kπ) · [ sin(u_k(d−a)) − sin(u_k(c−a)) ], ψ_0 = d − c

For a put, Vk = (2/(b−a))·K·[ψk(a,0) − χk(a,0)]. Note the two structural facts the hardware exploits: χ needs a reciprocal of (1+uk²), and ψ needs 1/k. Both become ROM lookups or Newton reciprocals later; neither needs a divider.

3.4 The truncation range

[a,b] comes from the cumulants of the log-price. The implementation (hardware/gen/heston.py, build_setup) uses:

x = ln(S₀/K) (log-moneyness) c1 = x + (r − θ/2)T + ((1 − e^(−κT))/(2κ))·(θ − v₀) c2 = max( v₀T + (θ/2)T , 2^(−FL) ) a = c1 − 10√c2 , b = c1 + 10√c2

The factor 10 is the standard L ≈ 10 safety multiplier; in RTL it is formed as (√c2 << 3) + (√c2 << 1) — two shifts and an add, no multiplier. The max(·, 2^−FL) guards against a degenerate zero range in fixed point.

A convention we inherit and must state in the paper. [a,b] depends on the parameters, but it is held fixed when differentiating — we do not propagate ∂a/∂θ etc. This is the standard COS convention and the software reference does the same, so the comparison is self-consistent. It is a known approximation, not an oversight.

3.5 Why this shape is what hardware wants

  1. N is fixed at design time (N = 128). Fixed loop trip count → a schedule can be computed offline.
  2. No branching. Call vs put is a mux on already-computed values, not a different code path.
  3. No random numbers. Deterministic and bit-reproducible.
  4. Terms are independent. Term k does not depend on term k−1. This is what allows software pipelining (section 10) — you can start term k+1 before term k finishes.
  5. Everything that does not depend on k can be hoisted out of the loop into a once-per-evaluation setup — the truncation range, 1/K, π/(b−a), e^a, e^b, ρξ, ξ², κ², 1/ξ², κθ/ξ².

Point 4 is the one that ends up mattering most, and point 5 is what makes the host-split variant of section 12 possible.

04AAD from scratch: why one backward pass gives every Greek

This is the intellectual core of the project. If you understand only one section, make it this one.

4.1 The three ways to get a derivative

MethodCost for n inputsAccuracy
Finite differences (bump-and-reprice)n + 1 pricingsApproximate — truncation and cancellation error, needs a tuned step h
Forward-mode ADn passesExact to machine precision
Reverse-mode AD (AAD)1 pass, all nExact to machine precision

Finite differences are worth dismissing properly, because they are the baseline we beat. Take a central difference:

∂V/∂p ≈ ( V(p + h) − V(p − h) ) / 2h truncation error ~ h²·V'''/6 (falls as h → 0) cancellation error ~ ε_machine · |V| / h (RISES as h → 0)

Two errors pulling opposite ways means there is an optimal h, it depends on the parameter and the machine precision, and you cannot know it in advance. In fixed point with ε = 2−32, the optimum lands around h ≈ 10−2…10−4 and the best achievable error is ~10−7…10−5. We measure exactly this in experiment E7.

4.2 The chain rule as a graph

Any numerical program is a sequence of elementary operations, so it is a directed acyclic graph: inputs at the leaves, one output at the root. Take a toy example:

f(x,y) = x·y + sin(x) at x = 2, y = 3 v1 = x · y = 6 v2 = sin(x) = 0.9093 v3 = v1 + v2 = 6.9093 = f

The chain rule says the total derivative is a product of Jacobians along every path from input to output. The only freedom is the order you multiply them in — and since matrix multiplication is associative but not commutative in cost, that order changes the arithmetic dramatically.

4.3 Forward mode: push derivatives from the inputs

Carry ẋ = ∂(·)/∂x alongside each value. Seed ẋ = 1, ẏ = 0:

v̇1 = ẋ·y + x·ẏ = 1·3 + 2·0 = 3 v̇2 = cos(x)·ẋ = cos(2) = −0.4161 v̇3 = v̇1 + v̇2 = 2.5839 → ∂f/∂x = 2.5839 ✓ (check: y + cos x = 3 − 0.4161)

Correct — but that pass gave us one derivative. To also get ∂f/∂y you re-run with ẏ = 1. For n inputs, n passes. Forward mode's cost scales with the number of inputs.

4.4 Reverse mode: pull derivatives back from the output

Define the adjoint of every intermediate variable:

v̄_i ≡ ∂f / ∂v_i "how much does the final output move if v_i moves?"

Seed the output adjoint v̄3 = 1 (the output's derivative with respect to itself), then walk the graph backwards. At each operation, push the adjoint to that operation's inputs, multiplying by the local partial derivative:

v3 = v1 + v2 → v̄1 += v̄3·1 = 1 v̄2 += v̄3·1 = 1 v2 = sin(x) → x̄ += v̄2·cos(x) = −0.4161 v1 = x·y → x̄ += v̄1·y = 3 ȳ += v̄1·x = 2 x̄ = −0.4161 + 3 = 2.5839 = ∂f/∂x ✓ ȳ = 2 = ∂f/∂y ✓

One backward walk produced every partial derivative simultaneously. Reverse mode's cost scales with the number of outputs, not inputs — and we have exactly one output (the price) and nine inputs we care about. That asymmetry is the entire reason this project exists.

The rule of thumb. Forward mode when outputs ≫ inputs. Reverse mode when inputs ≫ outputs. Option pricing is the extreme case of the latter: one price, many risk factors. Giles & Glasserman's "Smoking Adjoints" (2006) made this argument for Monte Carlo; we make it for a deterministic Fourier pricer, in hardware.

Look at the right-hand column carefully. Every adjoint rule reuses a quantity the forward pass already computed — 1/b for division, v itself for exp and sqrt. In software that is a minor optimisation. In our hardware, where a complex reciprocal costs about ten multiplier slots, it is the difference between fitting on a Zynq-7020 and not. Section 9.4 returns to this.

4.6 Complex adjoints

The characteristic function is complex, but the price is real, so we need the convention for pushing a real-valued output's adjoint back through complex arithmetic. For a holomorphic w = f(z), treating the adjoint as a cotangent vector gives:

z̄ += conj( ∂w/∂z ) · w̄ so for w = z1·z2 : z̄1 += conj(z2)·w̄ ("cmul with a conjugate") for w = z1/z2 : z̄1 += w̄/conj(z2) , z̄2 −= conj(w/z2)·w̄

This is exactly what the code does — P.cmul(g, P.conj(g, dratio), aD) and P.cdiv_inv(g, a_Dratio, P.conj_inv(g, inv_omge)) in heston.py. Note conj_inv: the conjugate of an already-computed reciprocal is free (negate the imaginary part), so the reverse pass gets its division for the cost of a complex multiply.

4.7 The tape — and why we delete it

Software AAD implements the backward walk with a tape (a Wengert list): during the forward pass, every operation appends a record of its type, its input indices, and its local partials. The backward pass then replays that list in reverse. This is what software/aad_engine/ does, via operator overloading, and what generation 1 of the hardware did with a BRAM.

idx | var | op | inputs | local partials ----+------------+-------+--------+---------------- 0 | x = 2 | INPUT | — | — 1 | y = 3 | INPUT | — | — 2 | v1 = 6 | MUL | [0,1] | [3, 2] 3 | v2 = 0.909 | SIN | [0] | [−0.416] 4 | v3 = 6.909 | ADD | [2,3] | [1, 1]

A tape is the right answer when the graph is discovered at runtime. Our graph is not. The COS method's operation sequence is identical for every input — same 128 terms, same operations, same order, with call/put resolved by a mux rather than a branch. So the tape is the same every time, which means it does not need to exist at runtime at all: it can be unrolled symbolically when the hardware is generated, and become wires and registers.

This is novelty point 1, stated early because everything downstream depends on it. We do not build a tape machine in hardware. We fuse the forward computation and its adjoint sweep into one static dataflow graph at generation time, then schedule that fused graph onto shared arithmetic units. There is no tape memory, no replay engine, no backward sequencing — the "reverse pass" is just more nodes in the same graph.

4.8 What this costs, in principle

The classical result is that reverse-mode AD costs a small constant times the forward evaluation — typically 3–5× in software, because of tape write traffic and the reread on the backward sweep. Our measured figure is 1.04× (4,731 cycles for price + 9 Greeks vs 4,565 for price alone, Zynq-7020 config). Section 10 explains why: with no tape traffic, the extra adjoint multiplies simply occupy multiplier slots that the forward pass was leaving idle.

Part III — The hardware

05Fixed-point arithmetic: the number system everything runs in

FPGAs can do floating point, but at a large cost in DSPs and latency. Fixed point is an integer with an implied binary point, so a multiply is an integer multiply and an add is an integer add.

Q(I.F): I integer bits (including sign) + F fractional bits, WL = I + F value = stored_integer · 2^(−F) ULP = 2^(−F) (unit in the last place — the resolution)

Two formats are used in this project:

ConfigWLFLUsed byWhy
z7 / z7h5628Zynq-7020 targetsNarrowest word that keeps Greeks below ~1e-4 relative; smaller words shrink LUT and DSP count.
zu6432UltraScale+ target, gen-1 engineFull precision reference; Q32.32.

5.1 Why 28 integer bits are needed

Not for the price — for the intermediates. The two magnitude peaks are |u_k|²ξ² inside the complex square root, which reaches 104–105 at N = 128 for short maturities, and the payoff coefficients |V_k|, which reach ~103. Accumulators get 8 extra integer bits.

5.2 Rounding is not a detail

A fixed-point product of two Q(I.F) numbers is Q(2I.2F); you must shift right by F to get back to Q(I.F). The obvious >>> FL is a floor, which biases every single product by −0.5 ULP. Over 128 terms, amplified by Vk ~ 300, generation 1 measured a −6.8e-7 absolute bias in the price from this alone. The fix is round-to-nearest everywhere:

rshift_round(v, s) = (v + (1 << (s−1))) >> s for s > 0 v << (−s) otherwise

This is ir.rshift_round, and it is applied at every rounding point in both the emulator and the RTL — which is why they can be bit-identical.

5.3 The defects that had to be found first

5.4 Adjoint normalisation — a trick worth understanding

At high k, |φ(uk)| decays to ~10−5. The adjoints seeded from that term are therefore only a few hundred ULPs — they have lost almost all their significant bits — and they then get multiplied by uk ~ 10². The result is a large relative error in a small number, which nonetheless contributes to the sum.

The fix uses a property of the reverse sweep: it is linear in the seed. So run it on seed·2s for a per-term shift s chosen to put the seed back in full precision, then shift the results back by 2−s at the end. Because both shifts are exact powers of two, nothing is lost. In the code this is g.adj_shift(wn_r, wn_i, fl) producing sh, and the final g.scale(node, nsh, fl) with nsh = −sh.

06Generation 1: what we built first, and why it was not enough

The repository contains two complete hardware architectures. Understanding why the first one failed is the cleanest way to understand why the second one looks the way it does.

6.1 What it was

hardware/verilog/heston_*.v is a hand-written finite state machine — about fifty states (S_X_DIV, S_X_LOG, S_CORDIC_WAIT, …) driving a library of arithmetic modules one operation at a time. For Black-Scholes there is also a genuine tape design: bs_forward_core.v writes 25 tape entries (value, two parent indices, two local partials) into a memory, and bs_reverse_pass.v sweeps it backwards generically. It works, and it is verified.

6.2 The three things that killed it

Area

Every * inside every state inferred its own multiplier. Yosys counts 159 64×64 multipliers in heston_top_level — roughly ten DSP48 slices each, against the 220 a Zynq-7020 has in total. It was time-multiplexed in control but not in datapath.

Latency

28 bit-serial divisions per COS term, each taking WL+FL cycles. These were 80 % of the 433,779-cycle evaluation.

No overlap

Terms ran strictly one after another. Term k+1 did not start until term k finished, even though they are completely independent.

6.3 What it nonetheless proved

Generation 1 is not discarded work, and it still ships in the repository and the test suite. It established three things the second generation relies on:

  1. The mathematics is right. Its Greeks match double precision to 3.0e-7 (price) and 5.4e-5 (worst Greek) over the 21-case grid.
  2. AAD beats bump-and-reprice on accuracy in fixed point, measured on identical hardware (experiment E7). That result is architecture-independent.
  3. The error-bound method works (section 13), and was developed against this engine.

It also gives the paper an honest "naive hardware AAD" datapoint: 433,779 cycles. Generation 2 is 92× faster than that on the same board class, and 271× faster in the 64-bit configuration.

07The generator: one description, five artifacts

Generation 2 is not hand-written RTL. It is a Python program that takes the Heston-COS price and its adjoint sweep as a single operation graph, and emits everything else:

heston.py (the datapath as an operation graph: setup, term, finish) │ ├── emulate() bit-accurate fixed-point golden model ├── eval_float() the same algorithm in double precision ├── error_bound() first-order bound for the price and every Greek ├── ranges.py measured shift ranges → narrow barrel shifters + range_err └── sched.py modulo schedule: M shared multipliers, CORDIC units └── rtlgen.py synthesizable Verilog ├── gen_tb.py self-checking testbench (bit-exact vs emulate) ├── wrappers.py AXI4-Stream wrapper, bump-and-reprice baseline └── host.py host-side setup/finish for host-split designs

7.1 Why a generator rather than RTL

Four reasons, in increasing order of importance:

  1. The golden model cannot drift. The emulator and the RTL are produced from the same graph, so "bit-exact against the reference" is a property that can be checked at every register on every cycle — 1,254 to 1,993 individual checks per design — rather than just at the outputs.
  2. The error bound is computed on the same graph that becomes hardware, not on a separate model of it.
  3. Design-space exploration is a loop. Word length, fractional length, multiplier count, CORDIC count and style, and host-split are all parameters. The cycles-vs-multipliers table in section 14 is one script, twelve configurations.
  4. A fused forward+reverse graph is not writable by hand. The reverse sweep of the Heston characteristic function is ~250 multiplies of conjugates and reused reciprocals. Deriving and scheduling that manually, in fixed point, correctly, is not realistic.

7.2 The IR

The graph is a list of nodes, each with an operation, arguments, a fractional format, and a part (setup, term or finish). Only three operation classes consume shared resources:

RESOURCE_OPS = { "mul": MUL, "crot": CROT, "cvec": CVEC } FREE_OPS = { "const", "in", "pick", "shl" } # shl by a constant is wiring

Everything else — add, sub, mux, compare, ROM lookup, variable shift — is "glue" and gets dedicated logic. This split is the whole basis of the cost model: the currency of the design is the multiplier slot and the CORDIC slot, and the scheduler's job is to spend that currency well.

After construction, ir.simplify runs constant folding and common sub-expression elimination over the fused graph. This is where reciprocal reuse across the forward/reverse boundary (section 9.4) actually gets found — it is not hand-written, it falls out of CSE on a graph where the forward and reverse passes are present simultaneously.

08Divider-free arithmetic: turning latency into multiplier slots

This is the section that most resembles "why we use a radix-N decomposition to get a butterfly". The governing idea: every transcendental function is rewritten so that its entire cost is a countable number of multiplications, because multiplications are the one resource the scheduler can share. Nothing here is new mathematics; the contribution is choosing the forms whose cost is expressible in a single currency, and then accounting for it exactly.

8.1 The transformation, stated once

BEFORE (generation 1) AFTER (generation 2) ───────────────────── ──────────────────── divider: dedicated area, Newton iteration: 6 shared multiplier uses WL+FL cycles serial sqrt: bit-serial, dedicated Newton on 1/√x: 9 shared multiplier uses exp/log: dedicated modules table + Taylor: 7 / 6 shared multiplier uses ────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────── 28 divisions/term = 80% of latency → 0 divisions; 251 multiplies/term

The reason this is a win is not that Newton is cheaper in isolation. It is that a multiplier use is schedulable and a divider is not. Once everything is measured in multiplier uses, one resource-constrained scheduler can trade all of it off against one number M — how many multipliers you are willing to pay for. That is what makes the design-space curve in section 14 exist.

8.2 Reciprocal: Newton-Raphson on a normalised mantissa

To compute 1/x, apply Newton's method to the root of f(y) = 1/y − x:

y_{n+1} = y_n − f(y_n)/f'(y_n) = y_n + y_n(1 − x·y_n) = y_n(2 − x·y_n) error: 1 − x·y_{n+1} = (1 − x·y_n)² ← QUADRATIC convergence correct bits DOUBLE each iteration

Quadratic convergence is the whole point: an 8-bit seed becomes 16, then 32, then 64 correct bits in three iterations. But Newton only converges in a neighbourhood, so first normalise:

x = ±m · 2^e with m ∈ [1,2) (norm_e finds e; scale produces m) 1/x = ±(1/m) · 2^(−e) (exponent handled separately, exactly) y₀ = ROM_recip_seed[ top 8 bits of m ] 256 entries: 1/(1 + (i+0.5)/256) repeat 3×: t = m·y ; y = y + y·(1 − t) ← 2 multiplies per iteration

Cost: 6 multiplier uses. The exponent path (norm_e, scale, sign mux) is glue. Note the code writes the iteration as y + y·(1 − t) rather than y·(2 − t) — algebraically identical, but it keeps the small correction term in its own product, which is better conditioned in fixed point.

8.3 Inverse square root, and square root for free

Apply the same idea to f(y) = 1/y² − x:

y_{n+1} = y_n (3 − x·y_n²) / 2 ← also quadratic normalise with an EVEN exponent so m ∈ [1,4), halving the exponent is exact: y₀ = ROM_rsqrt_seed[ idx(m, 8, span4) ] 256 entries at 1/64 resolution repeat 3×: y2 = y·y ; my2 = m·y2 ; y = y·(3 − my2) · 2^(−1) ← 3 multiplies 1/√x = y · 2^(−e/2) COST: 9 multiplies √x = x · (1/√x) COST: 9 + 1 = 10 multiplies

Getting √x as x·(1/√x) rather than by a separate algorithm is the standard trick, and it is exactly why the inverse square root is the primitive rather than the square root.

8.4 Exponential: Tang's method

Range-reduce so the polynomial only has to be accurate on a tiny interval:

n = round( 32x / ln2 ) ← 1 multiply, result is an integer r = x − n·(ln2/32) ← 1 multiply ; |r| ≤ ln2/64 ≈ 0.0108 exp(x) = 2^(n≫5) · 2^((n&31)/32) · P(r) 2^((n&31)/32) ← ROM "exp2", 32 entries P(r) = Σ r^i/i! , degree 4 (degree 5 if FL > 36), by Horner ← 4 multiplies final scaling by 2^(n≫5) ← exact shift

Why degree 4 suffices: on |r| ≤ ln2/64 the first neglected term is r⁵/120 ≈ 1.2×10−12, which is below one ULP at FL = 28 and FL = 32. The 32-entry table is what buys that — it splits the exponent's fractional part off so the polynomial argument is 1/64 of a decade rather than a full one. Cost: 1 + 1 + 4 + 1 = 7 multiplies.

8.5 Logarithm: table plus Taylor

x = m · 2^e , m ∈ [1,2) j = top 6 bits of m ; c_j = 1 + (2j+1)/128 ← 64-entry table t = m · (1/c_j) − 1 ← |t| ≤ 1/129 ln(1+t) = t·(1 − t/2 + t²/3 − t³/4 + …) ← degree 4, Horner ln(x) = e·ln2 + ln(c_j) + ln(1+t)

The 64-entry table shrinks |t| from ≤ 0.5 (a bare mantissa) to ≤ 1/129, which is what lets a degree-4 series reach sub-ULP. Generation 1's bug was exactly this: a 5-term series on |t| ≤ 0.414, giving 5.2e-4 relative error. Cost: 6 multiplies (1 for m·(1/cj), 4 Horner, 1 for e·ln2).

8.6 Sine and cosine: CORDIC

CORDIC computes a rotation using only shifts and adds. Start with a vector and rotate it by a sequence of fixed angles arctan(2−i), each applied in one direction or the other:

x_{i+1} = x_i − σ_i · y_i · 2^(−i) y_{i+1} = y_i + σ_i · x_i · 2^(−i) σ_i = ±1 chosen to drive the residual angle to 0 z_{i+1} = z_i − σ_i · arctan(2^(−i)) after n iterations: (x,y) → K·(cos z₀, sin z₀) , K = Π √(1 + 2^(−2i)) ≈ 1.6468

Each iteration is a shift and an add — no multiplier at all. The gain K is a known constant, folded into the initial value or corrected afterwards. Two modes are used:

Latency is FL + 4 cycles (a load/fold cycle, FL+2 iterations, an output rounding cycle) — so 32 cycles at FL = 28. Remember that number; section 10.3 turns on it.

09Complex primitives: where fixed point actually bites

The characteristic function is complex, and naive complex arithmetic loses precision in ways that do not show up until you look at a specific Greek. Each of these is a fix for a measured defect.

9.1 Complex reciprocal — pre-scale before squaring

The textbook form is 1/b = conj(b)/|b|². The trap: forming |b|² = br² + bi² squares the magnitude, so if |b| is small you lose 2·log₂(1/|b|) bits before you have done anything. In Heston the denominator involves ξ², so |b|² involves ξ⁴ = 0.0081 for ξ = 0.3 — about 7 bits gone. Measured effect on generation 1: ∂V/∂ξ wrong by 1.9e-6 relative.

The fix is to normalise first so the squaring cannot lose anything:

p = norm_e( max(|b_r|, |b_i|) ) + 1 → max(|b_r|,|b_i|)·2^(−p) ∈ [0.5, 1) br = b_r · 2^(−p) , bi = b_i · 2^(−p) n2 = br² + bi² ∈ [0.25, 2) ← no bits lost, no overflow (y, e) = recip(n2) 1/b = ( br·y , −bi·y ) · 2^(e − p)

Cost: 2 (squares) + 6 (reciprocal) + 2 (final products) = ~10 multiplies. The exponent bookkeeping is all glue.

9.2 Complex square root — via CORDIC vectoring, not squaring

The principal square root of z is normally written with |z| = √(zr² + zi²), which has the same squaring problem and overflows at short maturity / high ξ, where |uk|²ξ² reaches 10⁵. CORDIC vectoring gives |z| directly, with no squaring at all:

|z| from CORDIC vectoring (one CVEC use) w = √( (|z| + |z_r|) / 2 ) ← via rsqrt: 9 multiplies + 1 other = z_i / (2w) = z_i · (1/√yv) · 2^(e−1) ← REUSES the same rsqrt result, 1 multiply if z_r ≥ 0: √z = ( w , other ) else: √z = ( |other| , ±w ) sign from sign(z_i)

Two things to notice. First, the z_i/(2w) form avoids the catastrophic cancellation of the textbook Im = √((|z| − Re)/2) at low frequency. Second, the division by 2w is not a division — it reuses the inverse square root already computed for w, shifted by one bit. That is the pattern this whole project runs on.

9.3 Complex logarithm

(K·|z|, arg z) ← one CORDIC vectoring ln z = ( ln(K|z|) − ln K , arg z ) ← ln K is a compile-time constant, folded in

The CORDIC gain K is absorbed as a subtracted constant inside the logarithm, so it costs nothing. Cost: 1 CVEC + 6 multiplies (the real log).

9.4 Reciprocal reuse — the structural win

This is the point where section 4.5's observation ("every adjoint rule reuses a forward quantity") turns into silicon.

The forward characteristic function computes several complex divisions. Its adjoint sweep needs the reciprocals of the same denominators — because ∂(a/b)/∂a = 1/b and ∂(a/b)/∂b = −a/b². Since forward and reverse are nodes in one graph, common sub-expression elimination finds this automatically. Each denominator is inverted exactly once and used by both passes:

1/den forward: g = num/den reverse: a_num, a_den g/den reverse only (the −a/b² term) 1/omg forward: ratio = omge/omg reverse: a_omge, a_omg ratio/omg reverse only 1/omge forward: dratio = ome/omge reverse: a_ome, a_omge dratio/omge reverse only 1/ratio = omg/omge ← not a new reciprocal at all: a multiply of two existing ones

That last line is the neatest one. The reverse sweep of log(ratio) needs 1/ratio. Rather than a fourth complex reciprocal (~10 multiplies), it is formed as omg · (1/omge) — a complex multiply, 4 multiplies. And conjugates, which the complex adjoint rules need everywhere, are free: conj_inv just negates the imaginary part of an already-computed reciprocal.

Why this is a hardware result and not a software one. In software, recomputing a reciprocal costs one division instruction and you would not think about it. Here a complex reciprocal is ~10 of the 251 multiplier slots in a term, and the term's slot budget is what sets II, which sets the entire latency. Fusing the graphs before scheduling is what makes the saving visible to the tool at all.

10Modulo scheduling: why all nine Greeks cost 1.04 pricings

Sections 8 and 9 reduced one COS term to a fixed bill: 251 multiplies, 3 CORDIC rotations, 2 CORDIC vectorings. This section spends that bill. It is the architectural heart of the project.

10.1 The problem

We have 128 independent terms and a fixed, small number of arithmetic units — say M = 8 multipliers. Running terms one after another wastes units whenever a term is waiting on a long dependency (a CORDIC takes 32 cycles, during which its multiplies have nothing to do). Running them fully in parallel would need 128× the hardware. The answer is the classic one from VLIW compilers and high-level synthesis: software pipelining via modulo scheduling.

10.2 The construction

Choose an initiation interval II. Term k starts at cycle S + k·II. A term takes L cycles to complete, so ⌈L/II⌉ terms are in flight at once, and in steady state every unit repeats its behaviour with period II:

term k uses unit u at phase (t − S) mod II ⇒ the binding is a MODULO RESERVATION TABLE of width II: for each unit, a set of phases it is busy in. A new operation may be placed at phase p on unit u only if p is free.
cycles_per_evaluation = length(setup) + (N_terms − 1)·II + length(term) + length(finish) z7h (host split, II = 32, L = 506, N = 128): 0 + 127·32 + 506 + 0 = 4,572 z7 (fully on-chip, II = 32): 4,731 zu (II = 8): 1,599

10.3 What sets II — and the coincidence that shapes the Zynq design

II cannot be smaller than the resource lower bound: for each unit class, total operations of that class divided by the number of units, times how long each occupies its unit.

II ≥ max over classes of ceil( uses(class) · busy_span(class) / units(class) ) busy_span = 1 for a pipelined unit (accepts a new op every cycle) busy_span = latency for an iterative unit (blocked until it finishes)

Now work it out for the two shipped configurations. At FL = 28 an iterative CORDIC has latency FL + 4 = 32, so it blocks its unit for 32 cycles.

z7 (56-bit, M = 8, 3 CROT, 2 CVEC, ITERATIVE CORDIC): multipliers: ceil(251 · 1 / 8) = 32 rotations: ceil(3 · 32 / 3) = 32 vectorings: ceil(2 · 32 / 2) = 32 ──── II = 32 ← all three classes balanced zu (64-bit, M = 32, 1 CROT, 1 CVEC, PIPELINED CORDIC): multipliers: ceil(251 · 1 / 32) = 8 rotations: ceil(3 · 1 / 1) = 3 vectorings: ceil(2 · 1 / 1) = 2 ──── II = 8 ← set by multipliers alone

The z7 numbers are not a coincidence — 3 rotation units and 2 vectoring units were chosen because that is what balances 8 multipliers at FL = 28. Adding a ninth multiplier would not help: II would still be 32, pinned by the CORDICs. The measured table in section 14 confirms exactly this — going from 8 to 16 multipliers on the z7 config moves the cycle count only from 4,731 to 4,619, a 2 % gain for double the multipliers.

10.4 Why the Greeks are nearly free

Here is the payoff, and the answer to "how can nine derivatives cost 4 % extra":

z7 config (56-bit, 8 multipliers)Cyclesvs price-only

The mechanism: on the z7 configuration II is pinned at 32 by the CORDIC units, not by the multipliers. The forward pass alone needs only ⌈(price-only multiplies)/8⌉ cycles of multiplier time, which is less than 32. So there are idle multiplier slots in every 32-cycle window. The reverse sweep's extra multiplies drop into those slots and cost no additional II. The residual 4 % is the longer term length L (the sweep extends the dependency chain), which only contributes once, not 128 times.

This is novelty point 2. The classical AAD cost result is "reverse mode costs a small constant × the forward pass", typically 3–5× in software because of tape traffic. In a modulo-scheduled hardware datapath with no tape, the adjoint sweep is close to free whenever the forward pass is latency-bound rather than throughput-bound — which is exactly what an iterative CORDIC makes it. Measured: 1.04×.

Note the honest form of the claim: on the zu configuration (II set by multipliers, pipelined CORDICs) the ratio is 1,599 / 1,023 = 1.56×, because there the multipliers are the bottleneck and the extra adjoint multiplies do cost II. Both numbers go in the paper.

10.5 Storage

With 16 terms in flight, a value produced by term k and consumed later must survive until its consumer runs — while terms k+1…k+15 are producing their own copies. The model:

a glue node owns its output register, which holds for II cycles a shared unit's output is valid for one cycle, captured into a register if used later a value alive for longer than II needs a CHAIN of ceil((life+1)/II) registers

This register chaining is the dominant flip-flop cost, and it is the biggest remaining optimisation: register sharing across values is not implemented, so every long-lived value has its own chain. Yosys maps many of these to SRLs (2,922 in the z7h design), which is why the utilisation figure must be quoted including SRLs.

13The error bound: AAD used twice

Most FPGA-finance papers validate fixed point by running many cases and quoting the worst observed error. That is an observation, not a guarantee, and it says nothing about inputs you did not try. This section derives a bound, per Greek, per parameter set — and the machinery that computes it is the same reverse-mode AD the hardware implements.

13.1 The setup

Model each RTL operation i as producing its exact value perturbed by a bounded error:

operation i produces v_i + e_i with |e_i| ≤ ε_i · 2^(−FL)

The εi are derived in each primitive's header from its algorithm — how many rounding points, what the polynomial truncation contributes, how the CORDIC gain behaves:

fp_log2.7

13.2 The bound

To first order, an output Y moves by the sum of each error times that error's sensitivity:

|Y_rtl − Y_exact| ≤ 2^(−FL) · Σ_i |∂Y/∂v_i| · ε_i (worst case) σ_Y ≈ 2^(−FL) · sqrt( Σ_i (|∂Y/∂v_i|·ε_i)² / 3 ) (typical: independent uniform) "bits lost" for Y = log₂( Σ_i |∂Y/∂v_i| · ε_i )

The worst case assumes every rounding error lands at its maximum and in the most damaging direction simultaneously — which never happens. The σ estimate treats them as independent uniform variables (hence the /3, the variance of a uniform on [−1,1]) and is what actually predicts the measured error.

13.3 The point: where the sensitivities come from

The bound needs ∂Y/∂vi — the sensitivity of each output to a perturbation at every internal node. There are thousands of nodes and ten outputs. Computing these by finite differences would be hopeless. But this is precisely the problem reverse-mode AD solves:

ONE reverse-mode sweep over the error-model graph, seeded at output Y, gives ∂Y/∂v_i for EVERY node i simultaneously.

And because the model graph includes the RTL's own reverse sweep as ordinary operations, the same machinery bounds each Greek, not just the price. So AAD appears twice in this project, at two different levels:

AAD as the product

Reverse-mode differentiation of the Heston-COS pricer, in fixed point, in hardware — this is what the chip computes.

AAD as the method

Reverse-mode differentiation of the error model of that hardware, in Python — this is how we certify what the chip computes, per Greek, in 0.1 s per parameter set.

validation/precision/heston_rtl_model.py models the RTL operation by operation, with the same operation order, the same branch decisions (Smith swap, guard bits, adjoint normalisation shift, payoff parity) and the same rounding points as the Verilog.

13.4 Scope — stated explicitly, because a bound with unstated scope is worthless

  • First order. Products of errors are O(2−2FL); at FL ≥ 20 all bounds are ≪ 1 so these are negligible.
  • Input quantisation excluded. Inputs are taken as the Q-format values actually applied. At Q32.32 representing r = 0.05 adds ~1e-8 to rho·Δr.
  • Overflow excluded. Range is checked separately (section 11).
  • Hardware error only. The bound covers the RTL relative to the double-precision COS algorithm it implements. COS truncation error — the method's own error against the true Heston price — is a separate series, reported separately.

16Novelty, explained from basics

Each claim below is written in four parts: the background (what a reader already knows), the claim (what is new), why it is not obvious (the reason someone competent had not already done it), and the evidence (which experiment supports it). A novelty claim without all four is just an assertion.

N1 — The adjoint sweep becomes a static datapath, with no tape

Background
Reverse-mode AD is implemented with a tape: the forward pass records every operation and its local partials into a growing list, and the backward pass replays that list in reverse. Every AAD library works this way, because in general a program's execution path depends on its inputs, so the graph is only known at runtime.
The claim
For a Fourier-cosine pricer the graph is input-independent — same 128 terms, same operations, same order, call versus put resolved by a multiplexer rather than a branch. So the tape does not need to exist at runtime. We unroll the adjoint sweep symbolically at generation time and fuse it into the forward graph, producing a single static dataflow graph that is then scheduled onto shared arithmetic. There is no tape memory, no write port, no replay sequencer, and no backward control flow in the hardware at all.
Why it is not obvious
Two reasons. First, the obvious hardware route is to build the software architecture in silicon — which is what generation 1 of this project did for Black-Scholes (bs_forward_core.v writes 25 tape entries to a memory; bs_reverse_pass.v sweeps them). It works, and it is slow, because a tape serialises exactly the thing hardware is good at parallelising. Second, the property that makes elimination possible is a property of the numerical method, not of AAD: it only holds because COS was chosen over Monte Carlo, whose graph genuinely does depend on the random path. The novelty is in noticing that the pricing-method choice and the differentiation-implementation choice are coupled.
Evidence
E2 — the generated RTL is bit-exact against the emulator at every register, for five designs, with no tape anywhere in the netlist. E8 — 4,731 cycles versus generation 1's tape-ish FSM at 433,779.

N2 — Nine Greeks for 1.04 pricings, because the adjoint fills idle slots

Background
The standard AAD cost result is that reverse mode costs a small constant times the forward pass — in practice 3–5× in software, dominated by tape write and re-read traffic. Bump-and-reprice costs n+1 pricings.
The claim
In a modulo-scheduled hardware datapath the adjoint sweep can be close to free. Measured on the Zynq-7020 configuration: 4,565 cycles for the price alone, 4,731 for the price plus nine sensitivities — 1.04×.
Why it is not obvious
It is not a property of AAD; it is a property of the scheduling. On that configuration the initiation interval is pinned at 32 by the iterative CORDIC units, not by the multipliers (§10.3). The forward pass therefore leaves multiplier slots empty in every 32-cycle window, and the adjoint sweep's extra multiplies drop into them without increasing II. The only residual cost is a longer term length, which is paid once rather than 128 times. Getting this requires scheduling forward and reverse together as one graph — schedule them as two phases and the slots are not there to fill.
Evidence
E8, the AAD versus price-only columns. And the honest counterpart in the same table: on the 64-bit configuration, where multipliers are the binding resource, the ratio is 1.56×. Both belong in the paper; the claim is conditional and we state the condition.

N3 — Fusing the graphs makes the reverse pass reuse the forward pass's reciprocals

Background
Every adjoint rule reuses a forward quantity: ∂(a/b)/∂a = 1/b, ∂(a/b)/∂b = −a/b², ∂exp(a)/∂a = exp(a). In software this is a footnote.
The claim
In a divider-free design a complex reciprocal costs about ten of the 251 multiplier slots in a term, and the term's slot budget is what sets II, which sets the entire latency. Because forward and reverse are nodes in one graph, ordinary common-subexpression elimination finds this reuse automatically: each denominator is inverted exactly once and serves both passes — 1/den, g/den, 1/omg, ratio/omg, 1/omge, dratio/omge. And 1/ratio is not a fourth reciprocal at all, but omg·(1/omge): a complex multiply, four slots instead of ten.
Why it is not obvious
The saving is invisible unless three choices are made first: no dividers (so reciprocals are expensive in the shared currency), fused graphs (so CSE can see across the forward/reverse boundary), and resource-constrained scheduling (so slot count maps to latency). Any one of them missing and this is a micro-optimisation rather than an architectural result.
Evidence
The per-term bill of 251 multiplies (§8.7), which is what II is computed from, and the schedule verification in E3.

N4 — A per-Greek error bound, computed by the same machinery the hardware implements

Background
FPGA-finance papers typically validate fixed point empirically: run a set of cases, report the worst error. That is an observation about the cases you ran.
The claim
Model each RTL operation as producing vi + ei with |ei| ≤ εi·2−FL. Then to first order every output satisfies |Y − Yexact| ≤ 2−FL·Σ|∂Y/∂vi|·εi. The sensitivities ∂Y/∂vi for every internal node come from one reverse-mode sweep over the error-model graph. Because that graph includes the RTL's own reverse sweep as ordinary operations, the same bound covers each Greek, not just the price — and it is computed per parameter set in 0.1 s.
Why it is not obvious
This is AAD applied one level up: the product is reverse-mode differentiation of a pricer; the validation method is reverse-mode differentiation of the error model of the hardware that does it. The reason it is affordable is exactly the reason AAD is affordable in the first place — thousands of internal nodes, one output, so reverse mode gets all the sensitivities in one sweep. Nobody would attempt this bound with finite differences.
Evidence
E5 — measured error / bound ≤ 0.21 over all 210 (case, output) pairs for generation 1 and ≤ 0.115 for the generated designs. E6 — the 1σ form of the same expression predicts the measured error across FL = 16…40, which is what shows the model is right rather than merely conservative.

N5 — Every transcendental reduced to shared-multiplier uses

Background
Fixed-point hardware normally implements division, square root, exp and log as dedicated modules — often bit-serial, always occupying their own area.
The claim
Rewrite all of them so their cost is a countable number of multiplications: reciprocal and inverse square root as Newton-Raphson on a normalised mantissa with an 8-bit ROM seed (quadratic convergence, 8→16→32→64 bits in three iterations); exp by Tang's method; log by a 64-entry table plus a degree-4 series; sin/cos by CORDIC (shifts and adds only). The result is a per-term bill of exactly 251 multiplies, 3 CORDIC rotations, 2 CORDIC vectorings.
Why it is not obvious
None of these algorithms is new — that is not the claim. The claim is that expressing everything in a single currency is what makes one resource-constrained scheduler able to trade the whole design against one number, M. A divider cannot be scheduled against a multiplier; six multiplier uses can. That translation is what turns a fixed architecture into a design-space curve, and it is why the Zynq-7020 fit could be found at all.
Evidence
E1 — every primitive within 0.5–3.7 ULP. Generation 1 comparison: 28 bit-serial divisions per term were 80 % of a 433,779-cycle latency; generation 2 has zero divisions. E8 — the whole design-space curve exists because of this reduction.

N6 — One description, five artifacts

Background
A hardware project normally has a reference model, RTL, a testbench, and an error analysis as four separate pieces of work that can silently disagree.
The claim
The operation graph is written once and produces the bit-accurate emulator, the double-precision evaluation, the first-order error bound, the measured shift ranges, and the synthesisable Verilog. They cannot drift.
Why it matters more than it sounds
It changes what "verified" can mean. Because the RTL and the golden model come from one source, the testbench can check every register at the cycle it becomes valid — 1,254 to 1,993 checks per design — rather than comparing ten outputs. And every property proved about the emulator is thereby a property of the hardware.
Evidence
E2, plus the verify_all.sh check that the committed hardware/verilog/gen/*.v is byte-identical to fresh generator output.

N7 — The baseline is bump-and-reprice on the same hardware

Background
It is easy, and common, to compare an FPGA implementation against a software baseline and report a large factor. Such comparisons mostly measure the platform gap.
The claim
Our baseline is 19 pricings with central differences on the same generated pricer, same word length, same arithmetic, same scheduler — heston_bump_z7 and heston_bump_zu, themselves verified bit-exact against 19 emulated pricings. The only variable between the two arms is the differentiation method.
Why it matters
It makes both results defensible: 18.3× fewer cycles, and more accurate than the best bump size chosen in hindsight per Greek. The accuracy comparison in particular would be meaningless across platforms, because the cancellation term ULP/h is a property of the number format.
Evidence
E7 and E8.

The one claim to be careful with. "First hardware implementation of AAD for option Greeks" rests on a literature search, and docs/05_literature_review.md flags the Weiss et al. (2016) FPGA-Heston citation as not verified — a September 2026 search did not locate a matching paper. Before the paper claims priority, that citation must be resolved, and the search repeated for recent preprints. The technically safer formulation is a claim about what was built — "a statically scheduled, tape-free hardware datapath for reverse-mode AAD, with a per-Greek error bound" — which is defensible on its own terms regardless of who was first.
