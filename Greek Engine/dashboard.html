Greeks Engine

Fixed-point FPGA hardware that computes a Heston option price and all nine of its sensitivities (the Greeks) in one forward and one reverse pass. It is generated from a single description, verified bit-exact against a golden model with a guaranteed error bound per Greek, and has run on a Zynq-7020 ZedBoard.

Novelty

The first FPGA implementation of adjoint (reverse-mode) differentiation for option Greeks, and the first FPGA implementation of the COS method: a generated fixed-point datapath for the Heston model that returns the price and all nine sensitivities in one forward and reverse pass, with a guaranteed error bound on every output, bit-exact on a Zynq-7020.

  1. Adjoint Greeks on an FPGA, for the first time. Every published FPGA Greeks engine re-runs the pricer once per sensitivity (Klaisoongnoen et al. 2022; AMD's Vitis finance library). No FPGA adjoint implementation exists in the 2024 survey of 99 FPGA option-pricing studies or in the 2024 review of 15 years of AAD in finance.
  2. The COS method on an FPGA, for the first time. COS pricing had run on CPUs and GPUs only; the only FPGA Fourier pricer (AMD) uses trapezoidal integration and returns the price alone.
  3. A tape-free adjoint datapath. Software AAD records a tape and replays it. Here the reverse sweep is compiled into a statically scheduled fixed-point datapath that shares its arithmetic units with the forward pass, because the COS pricer runs the same operations for every input.
  4. Nine Greeks for 1.04× the cost of the price. 4,733 cycles against 4,568 for the price alone. On the measured CPU, hand-derived Greeks cost 1.9×, AD tools 5.3–6.7×, and bump-and-reprice 19×.
  5. A guaranteed error bound for every Greek of a fixed-point adjoint datapath. Measured error never exceeds 0.103 of the bound over 21 cases, and 0.268 over 50 random cases on silicon.
  6. Bit-exact Greeks on silicon. 450 of 450 results identical to the model on a ZedBoard, 50 random cases back to back.
  7. A fair baseline. Against bump-and-reprice on identical hardware: 18.4× fewer cycles and 32–560× more accurate than the best bump size for each Greek.

Scope of the novelty

  • Reverse-mode differentiation itself is established: it is backpropagation, which neural-network training hardware already runs. What is new is its use for option Greeks on an FPGA, as a fixed-point pricing datapath.
  • The error-bound method is Linnainmaa's first-order rounding analysis (1976); what is new is bounding every Greek of a datapath that itself performs the adjoint.
  • Newton-Raphson, Tang's exp and CORDIC are standard arithmetic; the contribution is the architecture built from them.
  • Hand-derived analytic Greeks on one CPU core (29 µs) are faster than the FPGA (65 µs), and several cores give far more throughput. The FPGA wins on Greeks overhead (1.04 pricings against 1.9 for the best software), the per-Greek error bound and estimated energy, not on speed.
  • Published FPGA Greeks work solves a different problem (Monte Carlo with early exercise), so no speed-up over it is claimed.

Raise these before being asked

  1. Energy is an estimate on both sides; no power was measured.
  2. The silicon results used the bitstream from before put-call parity; the rebuild and repeat are pending.
  3. Timing closes at about 79 MHz, not 100 MHz; the board runs at 70 MHz.
  4. The reverse sweep is written by hand as IR operations; making the generator derive it is the next task.
  5. The host steps (setup and finish) still run on the PC, not the Zynq's ARM cores.
Why

Motivation

Risk needs derivatives, not just prices

A bank holding options must continuously know how each price moves with every input: spot, strike, maturity, rate and each model parameter. These sensitivities are the Greeks. Under the Heston stochastic-volatility model there is no closed-form price, so it is computed numerically; this project uses the Fourier-cosine (COS) method with 128 terms.

The usual method is slow and inaccurate

Bump-and-reprice nudges each parameter and prices again. Nine Greeks cost 19 pricings with central differences, and the result depends on a bump size with no safe choice: too small and rounding dominates, too large and truncation does. On this hardware its best case is still 10–1000× less accurate than AAD.

AAD is the fix, but only in software

Reverse-mode algorithmic differentiation (AAD) gets every Greek from one backward sweep over the pricing computation, at a small constant multiple of one pricing. Software AAD records a tape and replays it, typically costing 3–5 pricings. A literature check (Sept 2026) found no published FPGA implementation of AAD for option Greeks, and none of the COS method: FPGA Greeks work so far re-runs the pricer.

The three claims that carry the paper

N1

AAD with no tape. COS pricing runs the same operations for every input, so the reverse sweep can be unrolled at design time into a fixed datapath: no tape memory, no replay sequencer. Evidence: generated RTL bit-exact on 5 designs, now on silicon.

N2

Nine Greeks for 1.04 pricings. On the Zynq-7020 design the iterative CORDIC units set the pace, leaving multiplier slots idle that the adjoint's multiplies fill. The condition matters: on the 64-bit design, where multipliers bind, the ratio is 1.55×.

N4

A guaranteed error bound for every Greek. AAD applied a second time, to the hardware's own rounding model, bounds the error of each output. Measured error never exceeds 0.103 of that bound on the grid, 0.199 on the 50 random cases. The method is classical (Linnainmaa, 1976); applying it to a datapath that itself computes adjoints is the new part.

Many strikes at once (design study, 1 Oct)

All strikes of one expiry share the COS grid, so one characteristic function and one reverse sweep per term serve every strike; each extra strike costs 16 multiplies and one rotation per term (about 7% of one option). Built into the generator (hardware/gen/chain.py), scheduled and emulated with accuracy matching the one-option engine; not built in hardware. Zynq-7020 style: 2 strikes 4,907 cycles, 8 strikes 8,701 (1,088 per strike); 64-bit/32 multipliers: 32 strikes 4,892 cycles (153 per strike). The CPU gains as much: 32 strikes in 122.5 µs on one loaded core (3.8 µs per strike), against 35 µs per strike for 2 strikes on the Zynq-7020 at 70 MHz. Strike sharing does not give the FPGA a speed lead. validation/results/chain_sweep.csv

Improvement found on 29 Sep

Calls through put-call parity

The 50-case board sweep showed calls carrying about 180× the rounding error of puts. A call's COS coefficients contain eb, which grows large over a wide truncation range and then cancels. The design now always computes the put and adds S₀ − K·e−rT and its derivatives on the host. Measured on the 22 calls of the sweep, against the independent Fourier-integral price:

Result 4

On the ZedBoard

Test method

Vivado drives the engine over the JTAG cable through a JTAG-to-AXI master: no processor software involved. The PC writes the inputs, starts the engine, polls for done, reads the 9 raw sums and compares them bit for bit with the emulator. The host then turns the sums into the price and 9 Greeks.

Sweep design

2 fixed cases plus 48 random draws over the verified input domain, 22 calls and 28 puts, using a seed the hardware's shifter ranges were not tuned on. Cases run back to back with no reset between them, so leftover state would show. The script was tested against a software model of the board with injected faults before use.

Application, 1 Oct

Hedging on real NIFTY data

12 trades, Sep 2025 – Sep 2026: each sells 20 lots of an at-the-money NIFTY call and hedges daily with futures until 0.10 years remain. Heston is calibrated each day to real quotes only (at least 50 contracts traded), across all monthly expiries; the model's prices were within 0.019% of the index level in a typical day (median). Hedge ratios come from the bit-accurate engine model. Profit and loss is after brokerage, STT, exchange, SEBI and stamp charges, GST, slippage and 30% tax.

Stated openly

Known limits

79 MHz, not 100 MHz

The engine misses 100 MHz by 2.6 ns on 64-bit carry chains. Latency figures use the measured Fmax.

Board bitstream is one change behind

The on-silicon results used the design before put-call parity. The new RTL passes all simulation checks and needs a rebuild and re-run.

Host steps run on the PC

Setup and finish are Python on the PC, not C on the ARM. The processor-based design exists as a script but is not built.

Power is an estimate

Vectorless, 12.5% toggle rate. No simulated-activity (SAIF) figure or board measurement yet.

The adjoint is hand-written

The reverse sweep is written as IR operations and verified, not derived by an AD tool. Say "adjoint differentiation" in the claim, or automate the sweep.

Bounded input domain

Verified for S₀ = 100, K 60–150, T 0.1–3, and ranges for each model parameter. Outside it the hardware raises range_err instead of returning a wrong value.
